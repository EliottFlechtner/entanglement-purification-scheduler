Long-distance quantum networks need repeaters that distribute high-fidelity Bell pairs despite photon loss and operational noise. In all-photonic repeaters built from half-repeater graph states (HRGS), entanglement purification can be applied during resource generation, after a single hop, or after the end-to-end pair is established, and the copy count and circuit used may differ at each stage. Choosing where to purify, how many copies to consume, and which circuit to apply is therefore a combinatorial scheduling problem that the source protocol demonstrates by a single hand-picked example but does not solve.

This thesis represents every fixed, non-adaptive purification schedule as a directed acyclic graph and evaluates it with a single bottom-up pass computing fidelity, success probability, latency, rate, and generation cost. The evaluator reproduces the source protocol's three canonical fidelity benchmarks to four significant figures. On top of this model, three search tiers, structured baseline enumeration, an exact span-partition dynamic program, and a beam search with an added same-span pumping operation, construct and compare candidate schedules under a shared generation-cost budget $E_{\max}$; every returned candidate is a structurally validated schedule evaluated by the same physical model, not a separate approximation.

At the source protocol's reference configuration, a schedule found by search at the same generation cost as the hand-designed baseline improves rate by $2.5\%$. Allowing the search to spend less than that cost yields a schedule using only $60\%$ of it with a $52.8\%$ higher rate. The more consequential result concerns feasibility rather than rate: once inner-qubit error, a form of noise the source protocol does not model, is introduced, the fixed baseline can fall below the required fidelity while a schedule re-selected by search at the same cost remains feasible. The same feasibility advantage holds, on average, across a sample of independently randomized per-hop heterogeneous networks. Further experiments characterize how this advantage varies with noise level, timing effects, and chain length, including the minimum generation cost for which any searched schedule remains feasible as the chain grows.

These results are existence claims within the schedule families and search limits implemented here, not proofs of global optimality: the beam search and its pumping operation retain only a bounded frontier at each stage, so a schedule that is not found is only absent from the families that particular search explored, not necessarily infeasible in general.
