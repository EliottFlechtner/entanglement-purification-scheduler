Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reported results experiment by experiment. This chapter synthesizes them into a smaller number of broader claims: when and why scheduling actually helps (Section~\ref{sec:disc:synthesis}), the scope within which any optimality claim here should be read (Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}), the heralded-versus-optimistic trade-off the searched schedules consistently exploit (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}), and the limitations of the model and search procedure (Section~\ref{sec:disc:limitations}).

\section{When Scheduling Matters}
\label{sec:disc:synthesis}

Three results generalize beyond the single $N=10$ reference configuration, subject to the search scope of Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}.

\paragraph{A feasibility advantage, not just a rate advantage}
Under the idealized noise model the source papers test, the paper schedule and the searched schedules are all feasible, and the gap between them is a modest rate improvement ($2.5\%$ at matched cost, Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline}). Introducing inner-qubit error, a form of noise the source papers do not model, changes this picture qualitatively rather than just quantitatively: the paper schedule can fall below the fidelity floor entirely, while a schedule re-selected by search at the same cost remains feasible (Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen}). In both cases the mechanism is the same: the searched schedule concentrates purification where noise has accumulated most, which a uniform, hand-designed schedule cannot do.

\paragraph{The advantage generalizes beyond hand-picked configurations}
A sample of 100 random per-hop heterogeneous networks confirms the same pattern beyond a single hand-chosen configuration: the searched schedule beats a uniform convention in 80 of 100 cases, by a mean of $20.37\%$ rate, and never does worse (Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous}). One deliberately constructed network, with a single hop assigned a $12\times$ higher inner-qubit error rate than its neighbours, sharpens this into the same infeasible-versus-feasible pattern reported above.

\paragraph{The advantage concentrates in an intermediate regime}
\label{sec:disc:nearoptimal}
The noise sweep (Section~\ref{sec:exp:edsweep}) and the Pareto frontier (Section~\ref{sec:exp:pareto}) show the same shape. Scheduling barely matters when the fidelity floor is easy to satisfy, whether because noise is low or the allowed cost is generous: most schedules are then feasible and their rates cluster together, and the searched frontier itself goes flat past a certain cost (already $C=60$ at the reference noise level). The advantage grows once the constraint starts to bind: rate improvement over the uniform baseline rises from near zero at $e_d=0$ to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$, with visible separation only once $e_d$ exceeds about $0.004$. Scheduling therefore matters most in an intermediate regime, tight enough to bind but not so tight that no searched schedule is feasible.

\paragraph{The same regime reappears along chain length}
The minimum generation cost for $F\ge0.9$ grows faster than the source paper's linear $10N$ formula over the tested range ($E_{\max}^{\min}\approx1.192\,N^{1.666}$, Section~\ref{sec:exp:budget}), first exceeding it at $N=28$; this fit is descriptive, not asymptotic, and the crossover is beam-width-sensitive. A self-contained result needs none of those caveats: at $N=10$, the paper's own budget already overspends by $2.0$--$4.8\times$ across the tested noise range. Both reflect the same cause as the noise sweep: a fixed fidelity floor becomes harder to sustain as the chain or the noise grows. Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope} revisits the budget-scaling result below, since it is where the search's own scope limits, not just physics, first become binding.

\paragraph{The search accepts other objectives with no new algorithm}
None of the results above needed a different search procedure. Replacing the objective, rate, fidelity, or cost, changes only which schedule is returned, not how the search finds it (Section~\ref{sec:exp:objectives}), because candidate construction and evaluation are kept separate throughout Chapter~\ref{chap:algorithms}.

\section{Optimality Scope}
\label{sec:disc:scope}

% Source: docs/Optimality Scope.md, Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

The beam-search tier (Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam}) includes one same-span pumping operation, with the candidate frontier bounded by the beam width. All returned schedules are therefore valid constructions within the searched families, not proofs of global optimality: finding the same best candidate at two beam widths is evidence of stability, not proof that no better candidate was pruned. An uncapped pumping mode (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}) is available for targeted validation but becomes impractical on larger instances, so it is used as a diagnostic rather than the default.

The excluded-construction result at $N=18$ is the clearest demonstration: a schedule built using a purification pattern outside the searched families is feasible at the paper's own budget, while the beam search reports a higher minimum (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}). This is exactly the chain-length result of Section~\ref{sec:disc:synthesis} read from the other direction: the minimum-budget curve grows faster than $10N$ for the searched families, but that curve describes what the search can find, not what is achievable in principle.

The results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} should therefore be read asymmetrically. Existence claims, that a feasible schedule was found, hold for the searched model, since the schedule is explicitly constructed, validated, and evaluated by the shared physical model. Non-existence claims apply only to the families and search limits actually explored; they do not prove no legal schedule exists outside that space.

\section{Heralded vs.\ Optimistic Execution}
\label{sec:disc:optimistic}

% Source: Validated Formal Model Def.md §2.6, sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

Section~\ref{sec:exp:timing} left the timing sweeps at the level of an observation: schedules respond differently to memory dephasing and generation latency, without saying why. The Herald-placement formalism of Section~\ref{sec:model:dag} supplies that mechanism as a genuine timing trade-off. An optimistic sequence defers heralding, avoiding intermediate classical round-trips, at the cost of only identifying a failed speculative operation after later operations have already been attempted. At the reference configuration, with no inner-qubit error and $\gamma=0$, the optimizer consistently favours optimistic candidates because the reduced communication delay outweighs the loss in success probability. When $\gamma>0$, heralded pumping is further penalized because its sacrificial copies sit in memory through each round-trip delay; optimistic execution never waits on an intermediate herald and so avoids this.

The two effects reinforce each other in the reference regime: the shape that reduces communication latency also avoids the memory waiting that would otherwise increase decoherence. This is not a universal preference: the balance depends on the relative communication delay, memory coherence, and failure probabilities, and different physical parameters could favour intermediate heralding instead. It should be read as a property of the tested configurations, not a general architectural rule.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}

% Source: Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

Four limitations bound the scope of the results.

First, only non-adaptive schedules are evaluated: the schedule is fixed before execution and cannot condition a later purification step on an intermediate outcome (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}). A fully adaptive strategy would need a different formulation; the results here bound only the non-adaptive schedule space.

Second, the search does not prune on memory footprint. $M_{\max}$, the number of branches held in memory at once, is computed exactly for every candidate (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}; quantified in Appendix~\ref{app:mmax}), but only the generation-cost budget $E_{\max}$ constrains which candidates the search keeps. A schedule reported here as feasible under $E_{\max}$ can therefore still need more simultaneously-held branches than a deployment with a strict memory limit could support.

Third, the fidelity model assumes Pauli-channel noise, consistent with the source papers. Comparisons between schedules are internally consistent under that model, but a ranking obtained here need not carry over to hardware dominated by a different error process, particularly one coupling loss, gate errors, and memory effects not represented here.

Fourth, the fixed-width frontier makes the search sensitive to which construction types compete for retained slots: enabling same-span pumping changes which candidate is returned at the reference configuration, so adding a new construction can change previously reported results even at fixed beam width. Extensions should include explicit before-and-after comparisons rather than assume a new construction can only help.
