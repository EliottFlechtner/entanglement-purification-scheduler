Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presented the experimental results individually: the headline comparison, network-generalization test, noise sweep, Pareto frontier, budget-scaling sweep, and timing sensitivity study. This chapter brings those results together into five design findings (Section~\ref{sec:disc:principles}), discusses when scheduling has little effect and when it becomes consequential (Sections~\ref{sec:disc:nearoptimal}--\ref{sec:disc:matters}), defines the scope within which optimality-related claims should be interpreted (Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}), examines the trade-off between heralded and optimistic execution (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}), and closes with the limitations of the model and search procedure (Section~\ref{sec:disc:limitations}).

\section{Five Design Findings}
\label{sec:disc:principles}

% Source: docs/Design Principles.md §Summary table

The experimental results support five findings that extend beyond the specific $N=10$ reference configuration, while remaining subject to the search scope and parameter ranges examined in this thesis. Taken together they place the thesis's central claim precisely: the formal model and search machinery are validated and produce real, non-uniform, working schedules, and the clearest evidence for their value is a feasibility advantage under changed physics (F1 below), not a rate-percentage win over a hand-picked example. Whether those schedules are globally optimal, or can be found faster, remains open and is scoped explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}.

\textbf{F1: Searched schedules stay feasible under changed physics; fixed schedules do not.} When non-zero inner-qubit error is introduced, the paper schedule falls to $F=0.6737$ in the 18-arm configuration and fails the $F\ge0.9$ threshold, while the optimizer finds schedules that remain feasible at the same $C=100$ budget in both non-ideal configurations tested (Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen}). The result is a feasibility advantage rather than a rate-percentage one: a fixed schedule chosen for one noise model can stop satisfying the fidelity requirement once the physics changes, whereas a search re-run against the new configuration can select a schedule that still clears it. A designed per-hop heterogeneous network reproduces the same pattern in a setting closer to a real deployment, where station spacing and hardware quality vary hop to hop rather than only across whole alternate configurations: every uniform link-level candidate fails the same floor, while a schedule shaped differently at each hop clears it at lower cost (Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous}).

\textbf{F2: Non-uniform allocation outperforms uniform allocation at equal cost.} The Pareto frontier at $N=10$ shows the paper's $C=100$ baseline lying inside the searched frontier: schedules found by the search reach $F=0.9351$ at $C=80$ and $F=0.9355$ at $C=100$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:pareto}). The matched-cost optimizer improves on the paper schedule by allocating purification unevenly, concentrating it where accumulated noise is greatest, rather than repeating one pattern across the chain. Whenever noise accumulates unevenly along a chain, non-uniform allocation can therefore use a fixed budget more effectively than a uniform strategy, though the present experiments do not establish this as a universal property of all network configurations. A sample of 100 independently random per-hop heterogeneous networks supports this beyond the hand-picked configurations above: the optimizer improves rate over the best feasible uniform recipe in 80 of 100 networks, by a mean of $20.37\%$, and never does worse (Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous}). The size of that improvement, however, is only weakly related to a simple coefficient-of-variation summary of how heterogeneous a given network is (Pearson correlation $0.020$), so this sample supports the direction of the effect more strongly than it supports predicting its magnitude from a simple heterogeneity measure.

\textbf{F3: The minimum feasible budget grows super-linearly with $N$.} Over $N=10$--28, the minimum budget found within the searched families follows $E_{\max}^{\min}\approx1.369\,N^{1.620}$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:budget}). The fitted exponent exceeds one, so the required budget grows faster than the paper's linear $10N$ formula over this range; the paper's budget is first exceeded at $N=28$, with the crossover bracketed between $N=26$ and $N=28$ and sensitive to beam width. A related, self-contained result at fixed $N=10$ needs none of that crossover's caveats: the paper's own fixed budget overspends by $2.0$--$4.8\times$ across the tested noise range (Section~\ref{sec:exp:budget}). Both are consistent with noise accumulating across additional hops, making a fixed fidelity floor progressively harder to sustain as the chain (or the noise level) grows. The $N$-scaling fit is descriptive rather than asymptotic and should be read as an observed scaling trend, not a general budget law.

\textbf{F4: Objective substitution does not require a new search procedure.} Replacing the objective, maximizing rate, maximizing fidelity, or minimizing cost, produces structurally different top-ranked schedules without changing the search algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:exp:objectives}), because candidate construction and candidate evaluation are separate: the search generates and retains candidates, and the objective only ranks them. One caveat: minimizing cost with only a fidelity floor can return a valid but low-rate schedule even when a higher-rate schedule exists at the same cost, so applications with an independent throughput requirement must impose an explicit rate floor.

\textbf{F5: Memory decoherence sensitivity depends on schedule shape.} Memory dephasing affects a schedule only when one branch must idle while another becomes ready for a later operation; a combine point whose inputs arrive at the same simulated time introduces no such term. At the reference configuration, the schedules selected by the optimizer stay insensitive to $\gamma$ across the tested range because the search favours optimistic schedules that defer heralding and so avoid the waiting pattern that collapses heralded pumping (Section~\ref{sec:exp:timing}). This is not a general immunity to memory decoherence; it follows from which schedules the search happens to prefer under the timing and noise parameters studied here.

\section{When Optimization Helps Little}
\label{sec:disc:nearoptimal}

% Source: outputs/sweep_ed_n10/README.md, Design Principles.md §Finding 1

At low outer-qubit error and moderate $N$, the fidelity floor is easy to satisfy and the minimum required budget is well below the paper's allocation, so raw, fixed, and searched schedules can all remain feasible and the choice of schedule matters less for the achievable rate. The $e_d$ sweep shows this transition directly: the optimizer's rate advantage over the uniform link-level baseline grows from near zero at $e_d=0$ to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:edsweep}). At $N=10$, the minimum budget to satisfy $F\ge0.9$ is $C=60$, $60\%$ of the paper's $E_{\max}=100$; beyond that, the Pareto frontier plateaus near $F\approx0.9355$, so additional resources buy little further fidelity within the searched schedules (Section~\ref{sec:exp:pareto}). This is also why swapping objectives can trade substantial cost for modest rate changes: once the fidelity constraint is comfortably met, the remaining feasible schedules differ mainly in how they spend the slack.

\section{When Optimization Matters}
\label{sec:disc:matters}

% Source: Design Principles.md §F3, Optimizer Status.md, Optimality Scope.md

Scheduling matters most in two regimes: when the physical noise model differs from the one a fixed schedule was chosen for, and when the chain length approaches where the paper's linear budget becomes insufficient. The first is qualitative: the paper schedule can become infeasible while a re-run search adapts to the changed configuration and stays above the fidelity threshold at the same budget. This first regime is not limited to switching between a handful of alternate configurations: a random sample of per-hop heterogeneous networks shows the same qualitative pattern, and the one designed adversarial example tested pushes it as far as outright infeasibility for the uniform convention (Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous}). The second is quantitative: at $N=28$, the minimum budget found within the searched families is $E_{\max}=283$, just above the paper's $280$, and the requirement grows faster than linear over the tested range. The $N=18$ excluded-move result (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}) qualifies this: it is also where the limits of the searched space become more consequential, since a hand-constructed schedule outside the searched families is feasible at the paper's own budget while the beam search reports a higher minimum. The regimes where scheduling matters most are therefore also the regimes where the present search is least able to establish global optimality.

\section{Optimality Scope}
\label{sec:disc:scope}

% Source: docs/Optimality Scope.md, Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

The beam-search tier (Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam}) includes one same-span pumping move, with the candidate frontier bounded by the beam width. All returned schedules are therefore valid constructions within the searched families, not proofs of global optimality. Increasing the beam width can improve coverage but does not establish exhaustiveness: finding the same best candidate at two beam widths is evidence of stability, not proof that no better candidate was pruned. An uncapped pumping mode is available for targeted validation (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}); it removes the beam cap on the same-span pumping pool within the configured circuit and copy-count limits, but becomes impractical on larger instances and is therefore used as a diagnostic rather than the default search procedure.

The excluded-move result at $N=18$ provides the clearest demonstration of this limitation: a schedule constructed using a move outside the searched families is feasible at the paper's own budget, while the beam search reports a higher minimum (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}). The results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} should therefore be read asymmetrically. Existence claims, that a feasible schedule was found, are valid for the corresponding searched model because the schedule is explicitly constructed, structurally validated, and evaluated by the shared physical model. Non-existence claims, that no feasible schedule was found, apply only to the schedule families and search limits actually explored; they do not prove that no legal schedule exists outside that search space.

\section{Heralded vs.\ Optimistic Execution}
\label{sec:disc:optimistic}

% Source: Validated Formal Model Def.md §2.6, sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

The Herald-placement formalism of Section~\ref{sec:model:dag} captures a genuine timing trade-off. An optimistic sequence defers heralding and so avoids intermediate classical round-trips, accepting that failed speculative operations may only be identified after later operations have already been attempted. At the reference configuration, with no inner-qubit error and $\gamma=0$, the optimizer consistently favours optimistic candidates because the reduction in communication delay outweighs the associated loss in success probability. When $\gamma>0$, heralded pumping is further penalized because sacrificial copies remain stored during each heralding round trip and so undergo memory decoherence; optimistic execution avoids this because it never waits on an intermediate herald.

The two effects reinforce one another in the reference regime: the schedule shape that reduces communication latency also avoids the memory waiting that would otherwise increase decoherence. This does not make optimistic execution universally preferable, since the balance depends on the relative communication delay, memory coherence, and failure probabilities, and different physical parameters could favour intermediate heralding instead. The observed preference for optimistic execution should be read as a property of the tested configurations, not a general architectural rule.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}

% Source: Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

Four limitations bound the scope of the results.

First, only non-adaptive schedules are evaluated: the schedule is fixed before execution and cannot condition a later purification step on an intermediate measurement or heralding outcome (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}). A fully adaptive strategy would require a different formulation. The results presented here therefore characterize the non-adaptive schedule space and do not bound what an adaptive strategy could achieve unless the comparison is explicitly restricted to that space.

Second, $M_{\max}$, the number of concurrently open branches, is computed exactly for an individual schedule but is not used to prune the search itself (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}; quantified further in Appendix~\ref{app:mmax}). The generation count $C(\Sigma)$ therefore captures only one part of the resource requirement during search; a schedule that satisfies the generation budget may still require a larger intermediate memory footprint if executed under a strict concurrency constraint.

Third, the fidelity model assumes Pauli-channel noise, consistent with the source papers. Comparisons between schedules are therefore internally consistent under that model, but a ranking obtained here is not automatically guaranteed to carry over to hardware dominated by a different error process, particularly one involving interactions between loss, gate errors, and memory effects that are not represented in the current model.

Fourth, the fixed-width frontier makes the search sensitive to which move types compete for retained slots. Enabling the same-span pumping move changes which candidate is returned at the reference configuration, so adding a new move can change previously reported results even when the beam width and other settings remain fixed. Future extensions of the search should therefore include explicit before-and-after comparisons under identical settings rather than assuming that adding a move can only improve the result.
