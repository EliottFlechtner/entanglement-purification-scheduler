Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reported results experiment by experiment. This chapter synthesizes them into a smaller number of broader claims: when and why scheduling actually helps (Section~\ref{sec:disc:synthesis}), the scope within which any optimality claim here should be read (Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}), the heralded-versus-optimistic trade-off the searched schedules consistently exploit (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}), and the limitations of the model and search procedure (Section~\ref{sec:disc:limitations}).

\section{When Scheduling Matters}
\label{sec:disc:synthesis}

Three results generalize beyond the single $N=10$ reference configuration, subject to the search scope of Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}.

\paragraph{A feasibility advantage, not just a rate advantage}
The clearest result in this thesis is qualitative, not quantitative. Under the idealized noise model the source papers test, the paper schedule and the searched schedules are all feasible, and the gap between them is a modest rate improvement ($2.5\%$ at matched cost, Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline}). Once inner-qubit error, a form of noise the source papers do not model, is introduced, the paper schedule can fall below the fidelity floor entirely, while a schedule re-selected by search at the same cost remains feasible (Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen}). A sample of 100 random per-hop heterogeneous networks confirms this beyond hand-chosen configurations: the searched schedule beats a uniform convention in 80 of 100 cases, by a mean of $20.37\%$ rate, and never does worse (Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous}); one designed adversarial network sharpens this into the same infeasible-versus-feasible pattern. The mechanism is always the same: the searched schedule concentrates purification where noise has accumulated most, which a uniform, hand-designed schedule cannot do.

\paragraph{The advantage concentrates in an intermediate regime}
\label{sec:disc:nearoptimal}
The noise sweep (Section~\ref{sec:exp:edsweep}) and the Pareto frontier (Section~\ref{sec:exp:pareto}) show the same shape. Scheduling barely matters when the fidelity floor is easy to satisfy, whether because noise is low or the allowed cost is generous: most schedules are then feasible and their rates cluster together, and the searched frontier itself goes flat past a certain cost (already $C=60$ at the reference noise level). The advantage grows once the constraint starts to bind: rate improvement over the uniform baseline rises from near zero at $e_d=0$ to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$, with visible separation only once $e_d$ exceeds about $0.004$. Scheduling therefore matters most in an intermediate regime, tight enough to bind but not so tight that no searched schedule is feasible.

\paragraph{The same regime reappears along chain length}
The minimum generation cost for $F\ge0.9$ grows faster than the source paper's linear $10N$ formula over the tested range ($E_{\max}^{\min}\approx1.369\,N^{1.620}$, Section~\ref{sec:exp:budget}), first exceeding it at $N=28$; this fit is descriptive, not asymptotic, and the crossover is beam-width-sensitive. A self-contained result needs none of those caveats: at $N=10$, the paper's own budget already overspends by $2.0$--$4.8\times$ across the tested noise range. Both reflect the same cause as the noise sweep: a fixed fidelity floor becomes harder to sustain as the chain or the noise grows. The excluded-construction result at $N=18$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}) qualifies rather than contradicts this: it shows the searched families, not the true schedule space, run out of budget first, the same caveat developed next.

\paragraph{The search accepts other objectives with no new algorithm}
None of the results above needed a different search procedure. Replacing the objective, rate, fidelity, or cost, changes only which schedule is returned, not how the search finds it (Section~\ref{sec:exp:objectives}), because candidate construction and evaluation are kept separate throughout Chapter~\ref{chap:algorithms}.

\section{Optimality Scope}
\label{sec:disc:scope}

% Source: docs/Optimality Scope.md, Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

The beam-search tier (Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam}) includes one same-span pumping operation, with the candidate frontier bounded by the beam width. All returned schedules are therefore valid constructions within the searched families, not proofs of global optimality: finding the same best candidate at two beam widths is evidence of stability, not proof that no better candidate was pruned. An uncapped pumping mode (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}) is available for targeted validation but becomes impractical on larger instances, so it is used as a diagnostic rather than the default.

The excluded-construction result at $N=18$ is the clearest demonstration: a schedule built using a purification pattern outside the searched families is feasible at the paper's own budget, while the beam search reports a higher minimum (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}). The results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} should therefore be read asymmetrically. Existence claims, that a feasible schedule was found, hold for the searched model, since the schedule is explicitly constructed, validated, and evaluated by the shared physical model. Non-existence claims apply only to the families and search limits actually explored; they do not prove no legal schedule exists outside that space.

\section{Heralded vs.\ Optimistic Execution}
\label{sec:disc:optimistic}

% Source: Validated Formal Model Def.md §2.6, sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

The Herald-placement formalism of Section~\ref{sec:model:dag} captures a genuine timing trade-off. An optimistic sequence defers heralding, avoiding intermediate classical round-trips, at the cost of only identifying a failed speculative operation after later operations have already been attempted. At the reference configuration, with no inner-qubit error and $\gamma=0$, the optimizer consistently favours optimistic candidates because the reduced communication delay outweighs the loss in success probability. When $\gamma>0$, heralded pumping is further penalized because its sacrificial copies sit in memory through each round-trip delay; optimistic execution never waits on an intermediate herald and so avoids this.

The two effects reinforce each other in the reference regime: the shape that reduces communication latency also avoids the memory waiting that would otherwise increase decoherence. This is not a universal preference: the balance depends on the relative communication delay, memory coherence, and failure probabilities, and different physical parameters could favour intermediate heralding instead. It should be read as a property of the tested configurations, not a general architectural rule.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}

% Source: Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

Four limitations bound the scope of the results.

First, only non-adaptive schedules are evaluated: the schedule is fixed before execution and cannot condition a later purification step on an intermediate outcome (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}). A fully adaptive strategy would need a different formulation; the results here characterize the non-adaptive schedule space and do not bound what an adaptive strategy could achieve.

Second, $M_{\max}$, the number of concurrently open branches, is computed exactly per schedule but is not used to prune the search itself (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}; quantified in Appendix~\ref{app:mmax}). A schedule that satisfies the generation budget may still need a larger intermediate memory footprint under a strict concurrency constraint.

Third, the fidelity model assumes Pauli-channel noise, consistent with the source papers. Comparisons between schedules are internally consistent under that model, but a ranking obtained here need not carry over to hardware dominated by a different error process, particularly one coupling loss, gate errors, and memory effects not represented here.

Fourth, the fixed-width frontier makes the search sensitive to which construction types compete for retained slots: enabling same-span pumping changes which candidate is returned at the reference configuration, so adding a new construction can change previously reported results even at fixed beam width. Extensions should include explicit before-and-after comparisons rather than assume a new construction can only help.
