Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reported results experiment by experiment. This chapter synthesizes them into a smaller number of broader claims: when and why systematic scheduling actually helps (Section~\ref{sec:disc:synthesis}), the scope within which any optimality-related claim in this thesis should be read (Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}), the heralded-versus-optimistic trade-off the searched schedules consistently exploit (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}), and the limitations of the model and search procedure (Section~\ref{sec:disc:limitations}).

\section{When Scheduling Matters}
\label{sec:disc:synthesis}

Three results from Chapter~\ref{chap:results} generalize beyond the single $N=10$ reference configuration, subject to the search scope discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}.

\paragraph{A feasibility advantage, not just a rate advantage}
The clearest result in this thesis is qualitative rather than quantitative. Under the idealized noise model the source papers themselves test, the paper schedule and the searched schedules are all feasible, and the gap between them is a modest rate improvement ($2.5\%$ at matched cost, Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline}). Once inner-qubit error is introduced, a form of noise the source papers do not model, the paper schedule can fall below the fidelity floor entirely, while a schedule re-selected by search at the same generation cost remains feasible (Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen}). The per-hop heterogeneous-network experiments extend this from a handful of hand-chosen configurations to a sample of 100 random networks: the searched schedule beats a uniform per-hop convention in 80 of 100 cases, by a mean of $20.37\%$ rate, and never does worse (Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous}); one designed adversarial network sharpens this into the same infeasible-versus-feasible pattern seen under non-ideal noise. In every case the mechanism is the same: the searched schedule concentrates purification where accumulated noise is greatest instead of repeating one fixed pattern everywhere, which is exactly what a uniform, hand-designed schedule cannot do.

\paragraph{The advantage concentrates in an intermediate regime}
\label{sec:disc:nearoptimal}
Both the noise sweep (Section~\ref{sec:exp:edsweep}) and the Pareto frontier (Section~\ref{sec:exp:pareto}) show the same shape. Scheduling barely matters when the fidelity floor is easy to satisfy, either because noise is low or because the allowed cost is generous: most schedules are then feasible and their rates cluster close together, and the searched frontier itself goes flat past a certain cost (already by $C=60$ at the reference noise level). The advantage instead grows once the constraint starts to bind: the rate improvement over the uniform baseline rises from near zero at $e_d=0$ to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$, with visible separation between schedules only once $e_d$ exceeds about $0.004$. Scheduling therefore matters most in an intermediate regime: tight enough that placement decisions bind, not so loose that every candidate is already feasible, and not so tight that no candidate in the searched families is feasible at all.

\paragraph{The same regime reappears along chain length}
The minimum generation cost for which a searched schedule reaches $F\ge0.9$ grows faster than the source paper's own linear $10N$ formula over the tested range ($E_{\max}^{\min}\approx1.369\,N^{1.620}$, Section~\ref{sec:exp:budget}), first exceeding it at $N=28$. This is a descriptive fit over the measured points, not an asymptotic law, and the crossover point is beam-width-sensitive. A related but self-contained result needs none of those caveats: at the fixed reference chain length $N=10$, the paper's own budget already overspends by $2.0$--$4.8\times$ across the tested noise range. Both point to the same underlying cause as the noise sweep: a fixed fidelity floor becomes progressively harder to sustain as either the chain or the noise grows, and that is exactly where a schedule's shape starts to matter. The excluded-construction result at $N=18$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}) qualifies this finding rather than contradicting it: it shows that the searched families, not the true schedule space, are what runs out of budget first, which is exactly the optimality-scope caveat developed next.

\paragraph{The search accepts other objectives with no new algorithm}
One further, more methodological point is worth stating briefly: none of the results above required a different search procedure for a different question. Replacing the objective, maximizing rate, maximizing fidelity, or minimizing cost, changes only which schedule is returned, not how the search finds it (Section~\ref{sec:exp:objectives}), because candidate construction and candidate evaluation are kept separate throughout Chapter~\ref{chap:algorithms}. This is what lets every experiment in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reuse the same three search tiers.

\section{Optimality Scope}
\label{sec:disc:scope}

% Source: docs/Optimality Scope.md, Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

The beam-search tier (Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam}) includes one same-span pumping operation, with the candidate frontier bounded by the beam width. All returned schedules are therefore valid constructions within the searched families, not proofs of global optimality. Increasing the beam width can improve coverage but does not establish exhaustiveness: finding the same best candidate at two beam widths is evidence of stability, not proof that no better candidate was pruned. An uncapped pumping mode is available for targeted validation (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}); it removes the beam cap on the same-span pumping pool within the configured circuit and copy-count limits, but becomes impractical on larger instances and is therefore used as a diagnostic rather than the default search procedure.

The excluded-construction result at $N=18$ provides the clearest demonstration of this limitation: a schedule built using a purification pattern outside the searched families is feasible at the paper's own budget, while the beam search reports a higher minimum (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}). The results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} should therefore be read asymmetrically. Existence claims, that a feasible schedule was found, are valid for the corresponding searched model because the schedule is explicitly constructed, structurally validated, and evaluated by the shared physical model. Non-existence claims, that no feasible schedule was found, apply only to the schedule families and search limits actually explored; they do not prove that no legal schedule exists outside that search space.

\section{Heralded vs.\ Optimistic Execution}
\label{sec:disc:optimistic}

% Source: Validated Formal Model Def.md §2.6, sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

The Herald-placement formalism of Section~\ref{sec:model:dag} captures a genuine timing trade-off. An optimistic sequence defers heralding and so avoids intermediate classical round-trips, accepting that failed speculative operations may only be identified after later operations have already been attempted. At the reference configuration, with no inner-qubit error and $\gamma=0$, the optimizer consistently favours optimistic candidates because the reduction in communication delay outweighs the associated loss in success probability. When $\gamma>0$, heralded pumping is further penalized because sacrificial copies remain stored during each heralding round trip and so undergo memory decoherence; optimistic execution avoids this because it never waits on an intermediate herald.

The two effects reinforce one another in the reference regime: the schedule shape that reduces communication latency also avoids the memory waiting that would otherwise increase decoherence. This does not make optimistic execution universally preferable, since the balance depends on the relative communication delay, memory coherence, and failure probabilities, and different physical parameters could favour intermediate heralding instead. The observed preference for optimistic execution should be read as a property of the tested configurations, not a general architectural rule.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}

% Source: Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

Four limitations bound the scope of the results.

First, only non-adaptive schedules are evaluated: the schedule is fixed before execution and cannot condition a later purification step on an intermediate measurement or heralding outcome (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}). A fully adaptive strategy would require a different formulation. The results presented here therefore characterize the non-adaptive schedule space and do not bound what an adaptive strategy could achieve unless the comparison is explicitly restricted to that space.

Second, $M_{\max}$, the number of concurrently open branches, is computed exactly for an individual schedule but is not used to prune the search itself (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}; quantified further in Appendix~\ref{app:mmax}). The generation count $C(\Sigma)$ therefore captures only one part of the resource requirement during search; a schedule that satisfies the generation budget may still require a larger intermediate memory footprint if executed under a strict concurrency constraint.

Third, the fidelity model assumes Pauli-channel noise, consistent with the source papers. Comparisons between schedules are therefore internally consistent under that model, but a ranking obtained here is not automatically guaranteed to carry over to hardware dominated by a different error process, particularly one involving interactions between loss, gate errors, and memory effects that are not represented in the current model.

Fourth, the fixed-width frontier makes the search sensitive to which construction types compete for retained slots. Enabling the same-span pumping operation changes which candidate is returned at the reference configuration, so adding a new construction can change previously reported results even when the beam width and other settings remain fixed. Future extensions of the search should therefore include explicit before-and-after comparisons under identical settings rather than assuming that adding a construction can only improve the result.
