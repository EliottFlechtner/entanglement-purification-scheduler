\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presented the experimental results individually: the headline comparison, noise sweep, Pareto frontier, network-generalization test, budget-scaling sweep, and timing sensitivity study. This chapter brings those results together into five design findings (Section~\ref{sec:disc:principles}), discusses when scheduling has little effect and when it becomes consequential (Sections~\ref{sec:disc:nearoptimal}--\ref{sec:disc:matters}), defines the scope within which optimality-related claims should be interpreted (Section~\ref{sec:disc:scope}), examines the trade-off between heralded and optimistic execution (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}), and closes with the limitations of the model and search procedure (Section~\ref{sec:disc:limitations}).

\section{Five Design Findings}
\label{sec:disc:principles}

% Source: docs/Design Principles.md §Summary table

The experimental results support five findings that extend beyond the specific $N=10$ reference configuration, while remaining subject to the search scope and parameter ranges examined in this thesis.

\textbf{F1: Non-uniform allocation outperforms uniform allocation at equal cost.} The Pareto frontier at $N=10$ shows that the paper's $C=100$ baseline lies inside the searched frontier: schedules found by the search reach $F=0.9351$ at $C=80$ and $F=0.9355$ at $C=100$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:pareto}). The matched-cost optimizer improves on the paper schedule by allocating purification unevenly rather than repeating the same purification pattern across the chain. The underlying reason is that purification is more valuable at locations where accumulated noise is greater. This suggests that whenever different parts of a chain experience different effective noise accumulation, non-uniform allocation can use a fixed budget more effectively than a uniform strategy. The present experiments do not establish this as a universal property of all network configurations, but they show that the benefit is not confined to a single fixed purification pattern.

\textbf{F2: The minimum feasible budget grows super-linearly with $N$.} Over the tested range $N=10$--28, the minimum budget found within the searched families is described by $E_{\max}^{\min}\approx1.369\,N^{1.620}$. The paper's linear $10N$ budget is first exceeded at $N=28$, with the observed crossover bracketed between $N=26$ and $N=28$ and sensitive to beam width (Section~\ref{sec:exp:budget}). The fitted exponent is greater than one over the measured range, indicating that the budget required to maintain the same fidelity floor grows faster than linearly in these experiments. This is consistent with the cumulative effect of noise across additional hops: maintaining a fixed end-to-end fidelity becomes progressively more demanding as the chain grows. The fit is descriptive rather than asymptotic, so it should be interpreted as an observed scaling trend over the tested range rather than as a general budget law.

\textbf{F3: Searched schedules stay feasible under changed physics; fixed schedules do not.} When non-zero inner-qubit error is introduced, the paper schedule falls to $F=0.6737$ in the 18-arm configuration and fails the $F\ge0.9$ threshold, while the optimizer finds schedules that remain feasible at the same $C=100$ budget in both non-ideal configurations tested (Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen}). The main result is therefore a feasibility advantage rather than a rate-percentage advantage. A fixed schedule chosen for one noise model can cease to satisfy the fidelity requirement when the physical configuration changes, whereas a search performed against the changed configuration can select a different schedule that remains feasible.

\textbf{F4: Objective substitution does not require a new search procedure.} Replacing the objective from maximizing rate with maximizing fidelity or minimizing cost produces structurally different top-ranked schedules without changing the underlying search algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:exp:objectives}). This reflects the separation between candidate construction and candidate evaluation: the search procedures generate and retain candidate schedules, while the objective function determines how those candidates are ranked subject to the relevant constraints. One practical caveat is that minimizing cost with only a fidelity floor can return a valid but low-rate schedule even when a higher-rate schedule exists at the same cost. Applications with an independent throughput requirement must therefore impose an explicit rate floor.

\textbf{F5: Memory decoherence sensitivity depends on schedule shape.} In the timing model, memory dephasing affects a schedule when one branch must remain idle while another branch becomes ready for a later operation. A combine point whose inputs become ready at the same simulated time does not introduce this additional wait-dependent decoherence term, whereas asymmetric arrival times do. At the reference configuration, the schedules selected by the optimizer remain insensitive to $\gamma$ across the tested range because the search favours optimistic schedules, which avoid the waiting pattern that causes heralded pumping to decohere (Section~\ref{sec:exp:timing}). This should not be interpreted as a general immunity to memory decoherence: it is a consequence of the schedules selected under the particular timing and noise parameters studied here.

\section{When Optimization Helps Little}
\label{sec:disc:nearoptimal}

% Source: outputs/sweep_ed_n10/README.md, Design Principles.md §Finding 1

At low outer-qubit error and moderate $N$, the fidelity floor is relatively easy to satisfy and the minimum required budget is well below the paper's allocation. In this regime, raw, fixed, and searched schedules can all remain feasible, so the choice of schedule has a smaller effect on the achievable rate. The $e_d$ sweep illustrates this transition: the optimizer's rate advantage over the uniform link-level baseline grows from near zero at $e_d=0$ to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:edsweep}). At $N=10$, the minimum budget found to satisfy $F\ge0.9$ is $C=60$, or $60\%$ of the paper's $E_{\max}=100$; beyond this point the Pareto frontier approaches a plateau near $F\approx0.9355$, so additional resources provide little further improvement in fidelity within the searched schedules (Section~\ref{sec:exp:pareto}). This also explains why the alternative objectives can trade substantial cost for relatively modest changes in rate: once the fidelity constraint is comfortably satisfied, the schedule space contains additional feasible choices that can be exploited by a different objective.

\section{When Optimization Matters}
\label{sec:disc:matters}

% Source: Design Principles.md §F3, Optimizer Status.md, Optimality Scope.md

The scheduling choice becomes most consequential in two regimes: when the physical noise model differs from the one for which a fixed schedule was chosen, and when the chain length grows toward a regime where the paper's linear budget becomes insufficient. In the first regime, the difference is qualitative: the paper schedule can become infeasible, while a searched schedule can adapt to the changed noise configuration and remain above the fidelity threshold at the same budget. In the second regime, the difference is quantitative: at $N=28$, the minimum budget found within the searched families is $E_{\max}=283$, slightly above the paper's $280$, and the observed requirement grows faster than the linear budget over the tested range. The $N=18$ excluded-move result (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}) adds an important qualification: this is also where the limits of the searched space become more consequential, because a hand-constructed schedule outside the searched families is feasible at the paper's own budget while the beam search reports a higher minimum. Thus, the regimes in which scheduling matters most are also regimes in which the present search is least able to establish global optimality.

\section{Optimality Scope}
\label{sec:disc:scope}

% Source: docs/Optimality Scope.md, Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

The beam-search tier (Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam}) includes one same-span pumping move, with the candidate frontier bounded by the beam width. All returned schedules are therefore valid constructions within the searched families, not proofs of global optimality. Increasing the beam width can improve coverage but does not establish exhaustiveness: finding the same best candidate at two beam widths is evidence of stability, not proof that no better candidate was pruned. An uncapped pumping mode is available for targeted validation (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}); it removes the beam cap on the same-span pumping pool within the configured circuit and copy-count limits, but becomes impractical on larger instances and is therefore used as a diagnostic rather than the default search procedure.

The excluded-move result at $N=18$ provides the clearest demonstration of this limitation: a schedule constructed using a move outside the searched families is feasible at the paper's own budget, while the beam search reports a higher minimum (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}). The results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} should therefore be read asymmetrically. Existence claims, that a feasible schedule was found, are valid for the corresponding searched model because the schedule is explicitly constructed, structurally validated, and evaluated by the shared physical model. Non-existence claims, that no feasible schedule was found, apply only to the schedule families and search limits actually explored; they do not prove that no legal schedule exists outside that search space.

\section{Heralded vs.\ Optimistic Execution}
\label{sec:disc:optimistic}

% Source: Validated Formal Model Def.md §2.6, sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

The Herald-placement formalism of Section~\ref{sec:model:dag} captures a genuine timing trade-off. An optimistic sequence defers heralding and therefore avoids intermediate classical round-trips, while accepting that failed speculative operations may only be identified after later operations have already been attempted. At the reference configuration, where inner-qubit error is absent and $\gamma=0$, the optimizer consistently favours optimistic candidates because the reduction in communication delay outweighs the associated loss in success probability. When $\gamma>0$, heralded pumping is further penalized because sacrificial copies remain stored during each heralding round trip and therefore undergo memory decoherence. Optimistic execution avoids this particular failure mode because it does not require the resource to wait for an intermediate herald.

The two effects therefore reinforce one another in the reference regime: the schedule shape that reduces communication latency also avoids the memory waiting that would otherwise increase decoherence. This does not make optimistic execution universally preferable. The balance depends on the relative communication delay, memory coherence, and failure probabilities; different physical parameters could make intermediate heralding more attractive. The experimentally observed preference for optimistic execution should therefore be interpreted as a property of the tested configurations rather than as a general architectural rule.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}

% Source: Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

Four limitations bound the scope of the results.

First, only non-adaptive schedules are evaluated: the schedule is fixed before execution and cannot condition a later purification step on an intermediate measurement or heralding outcome (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}). A fully adaptive strategy would require a different formulation. The results presented here therefore characterize the non-adaptive schedule space and do not bound what an adaptive strategy could achieve unless the comparison is explicitly restricted to that space.

Second, $M_{\max}$, the number of concurrently open branches, is computed exactly for an individual schedule but is not used to prune the search itself (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}). The generation count $C(\Sigma)$ therefore captures only one part of the resource requirement during search; a schedule that satisfies the generation budget may still require a larger intermediate memory footprint if executed under a strict concurrency constraint.

Third, the fidelity model assumes Pauli-channel noise, consistent with the source papers. Comparisons between schedules are therefore internally consistent under that model, but a ranking obtained here is not automatically guaranteed to carry over to hardware dominated by a different error process, particularly one involving interactions between loss, gate errors, and memory effects that are not represented in the current model.

Fourth, the fixed-width frontier makes the search sensitive to which move types compete for retained slots. Enabling the same-span pumping move changes which candidate is returned at the reference configuration, so adding a new move can change previously reported results even when the beam width and other settings remain fixed. Future extensions of the search should therefore include explicit before-and-after comparisons under identical settings rather than assuming that adding a move can only improve the result.
