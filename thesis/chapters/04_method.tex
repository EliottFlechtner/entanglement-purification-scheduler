Chapter~\ref{chap:related} showed that the source paper
\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} demonstrates purification schedules that
mix generation, link-level, and end-node purification, but only through one
hand-picked example and leaves the search for a better schedule as an open
problem. Turning this into a problem that can actually be searched requires a
precise object to search over. This chapter builds that object step by step:
the given physical network configuration (Section~\ref{sec:model:network}), the state carried by
each partially-built Bell pair (Section~\ref{sec:model:state}), the schedule
itself as a graph of operations (Section~\ref{sec:model:dag}), the resources it
is allowed to consume (Section~\ref{sec:model:budget}), and finally the
optimization problem this thesis \TOFIX{decide}{aims to solve/solves} (Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}).
Section~\ref{sec:model:scope} closes the chapter by stating precisely which
schedules are excluded from this model and why.

The building blocks introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:background}, namely the
error vector $\mathbf{e}$, the three purification circuits, and the
decoherence law, are not redefined here but reused in what follows. This chapter
is only concerned with how those pieces are assembled into a schedule and
how schedules are compared.

\section{Network Configuration}
\label{sec:model:network}

% Source: NetworkConfig / HopConfig classes, Integrating §V-A,
%          Bridging §III, §VI-A

A network configuration fixes everything about the physical link that a
schedule cannot change. Stations are numbered $0,1,\dots,N$ along the chain,
so a hop is the link between two consecutive stations $i-1$ and $i$: an
$N$-hop chain has $N$ hops but $N+1$ stations. With $i=1,\dots,N$ indexing the hops, the
configuration is the tuple
\[
    \mathcal{N} = \Bigl(N,\ \{\ell_i\},\ \{\vec{b}^{(i)}\},\
    \{k_i\},\ \bigl\{(p_{\mathrm{in},i}^{X}, p_{\mathrm{in},i}^{Z})\bigr\},\
    \{\eta_i\},\ e_d,\ \gamma,\ c\Bigr),
\]
where, for each hop $i$: $\ell_i$ is its length, $\vec{b}^{(i)}$ is the
tree-code branching vector used to build its half-RGS, $k_i$ is its number of
redundant \gls{bsm} arms, $p_{\mathrm{in},i}^{X}$ and $p_{\mathrm{in},i}^{Z}$
are its inner-qubit measurement error rates, and $\eta_i$ is its photon
survival probability. The remaining three parameters are shared across the
whole chain rather than defined per hop: $e_d$ is the outer-photon
depolarizing rate, $\gamma$ is the memory dephasing rate, and $c$ is the
speed of light in the fiber.
Per-arm \gls{bsm} success is capped at 50\%, independent of $\eta_i$ or
$e_d$\TOCITE{recheck integrating para + find where that comes from}; this
cap is exactly why each hop is built from $k_i$ redundant arms rather than a
single attempt.

\section{Representing a Partial Bell Pair}
\label{sec:model:state}

% Source: Validated Formal Model Def.md §3.1, schedule/state.py

At any point during its construction, a partially-built Bell pair is
represented by a state
\[
    S = \bigl(\mathbf{e},\; s,\; t,\; t_{\mathrm{gen}},\; \kappa,\; r,\; h\bigr).
\]
The vector $\mathbf{e}$ is the error vector introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:bg:errorvec}, and $s$ is the Z side-effect parity from
Section~\ref{sec:bg:sideffects}. The remaining four fields are new to the
scheduling layer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $t$ and $t_{\mathrm{gen}}$ are the current simulated time and the
          time this particular resource was generated. Their difference is the
          idle time used by the decoherence law of
          Section~\ref{sec:bg:decoherence}.
    \item $\kappa$ is the \emph{span} of the resource: either \texttt{RGSS} for
          a resource that has not yet crossed a hop, or $\mathrm{Span}(a,b)$
          for an edge that already connects station $a$ to station $b$.
    \item $r$ counts how many purification rounds have already been applied to
          this branch.
    \item $h \in \{\mathit{pending}, \mathit{resolved}\}$ records whether the
          classical heralding signal for this resource has been received yet.
\end{itemize}
The field that matters most for what a schedule is allowed to do is $\kappa$:
two states can only be combined by a purification circuit if they carry the
\emph{same} $\kappa$. Two branches with different histories, one purified
twice and the other purified once for instance, can still be combined as long
as both currently sit at the same span. This single rule is what lets the
model express schedules that mix purification strategies across different
parts of the chain, which is exactly the kind of schedule the source paper's
Fig.\,4 example uses.

\section{Schedules as Directed Acyclic Graphs}
\label{sec:model:dag}

% Source: Validated Formal Model Def.md §4, schedule/node.py,
%          Justification of Implementation.md §5.1

A schedule is written as a pair $\Sigma = (T, \phi)$, where $T$ is a rooted,
directed, acyclic graph and $\phi$ maps every node of $T$ to the span $\kappa$
of the state it produces. The leaves of $T$ are always \texttt{Gen} nodes
(half-RGS generation) and the root is always a \texttt{PauliCorrect} node at
$\kappa = \mathrm{Span}(0,N)$, the finished end-to-end pair.
Between leaves and root, five more node types appear:
\texttt{AbsaBsm} (the photonic Bell-state measurement that turns two
same-hop half-RGSs into a single-hop edge),
\texttt{Join} (entanglement swapping, stitching two adjacent spans into a
wider one),
\texttt{Purify} (a two-to-one YY, ZX, or XZ circuit, Section~\ref{sec:bg:circuits}),
\texttt{Herald} (resolving the classical heralding signal for a branch), and
\texttt{Idle} (an explicit wait, used only where the schedule's timing
requires one).

A graph $T$ is a \emph{legal} schedule only if four conditions hold:
\texttt{Gen} nodes may only appear at $\kappa = \mathtt{RGSS}$;
a \texttt{Purify} node's two children must share the same $\kappa$;
a \texttt{Join} node's two children must be adjacent spans, i.e.\ $(a,b)$ and
$(b,d)$, and it then produces $(a,d)$;
and the root must sit at $\kappa=\mathrm{Span}(0,N)$.
These four rules, together with the span-sharing constraint from
Section~\ref{sec:model:state}, are the complete legality specification: any
graph satisfying them is a schedule the physical hardware described in
Section~\ref{sec:model:network} could actually run.

This representation was checked against the source paper's own Fig.\,4
schedule, which builds one Bell pair from purified individual links, a second
from two purified 5-hop segments, and a third with no purification at all,
then combines all three with a final purification round at the end nodes. This
is a mixed-granularity, mixed-history schedule, and it is exactly the class of
graph this model can express: allowing $\kappa$ to be an arbitrary span, and
requiring \texttt{Purify} to check only $\kappa$ and not provenance, is what
makes that combination legal.

\section{Resource Budget}
\label{sec:model:budget}

% Source: Validated Formal Model Def.md §5, ResourceBudget class

A schedule is only meaningful together with a budget
\[
    B = (n_{\mathrm{pur}},\ e_{\max},\ M_{\max}),
\]
where $n_{\mathrm{pur}}$ is the number of extra half-RGS copies generated per
purification round, $e_{\max}$ is the maximum number of \texttt{Gen} leaves
$T$ is allowed to contain, and $M_{\max}$ is the maximum number of branches
that may be under construction at the same time.
A schedule $\Sigma$ is \emph{feasible} with respect to $B$ if it uses at most
$e_{\max}$ \texttt{Gen} leaves and never holds more than $M_{\max}$ branches
open at once. The resource cost $C(\Sigma)$ used throughout
Chapter~\ref{chap:results} is simply the number of \texttt{Gen} leaves in $T$,
so feasibility with respect to $e_{\max}$ is the same as $C(\Sigma) \le
e_{\max}$.

The source paper's own budget convention is $e_{\max} = 10N$: five half-RGS
copies per side, on both sides, for every hop. This convention is used as the
reference budget throughout the experiments, and Section~\ref{sec:exp:budget}
asks directly whether it remains large enough as $N$ grows.
$M_{\max}$ is part of the formal budget but, as stated in
Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}, it is not currently enforced by the search or
by the DAG validator; every reported result should be read with this in mind.

\section{Objective Functions and the Optimization Problem}
\label{sec:model:objectives}

% Source: cost_functions.py, Validated Formal Model Def.md §6,
%         Optimizer Status.md §Shared Optimization Contract

Four quantities can be read off the finished schedule DAG by a single
bottom-up pass: fidelity $F(\Sigma)$, the $w$ component of the error vector at
the root; rate $R(\Sigma) = P_{\mathrm{success}} / T_{\mathrm{gen}}$, combining
the schedule's overall success probability with its expected generation time
under a renewal argument (full restart on failure); resource cost $C(\Sigma)$,
defined in Section~\ref{sec:model:budget}; and latency $L(\Sigma)$, the
makespan of $T$.

The optimization problem this thesis addresses is then
\[
    \Sigma^{*}(\mathcal{N}, B) = \argmax_{\substack{\Sigma \text{ legal w.r.t. } \mathcal{N} \\ \Sigma \text{ feasible w.r.t. } B}}
    R(\Sigma;\mathcal{N}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad F(\Sigma;\mathcal{N}) \ge F_{\min}.
\]
In words: among every legal, feasible schedule, find the one with the highest
rate that still clears a minimum fidelity requirement. This is the objective
used in every headline result in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} unless stated
otherwise, with $F_{\min}=0.9$ matching the source paper's own quality bar.

The same four cost functions support other research questions by swapping
which one is maximized and which are constrained: maximizing $F$ subject to a
rate floor asks for the best achievable quality at a given throughput;
minimizing $C$ subject to floors on $F$ and $R$ asks for the cheapest hardware
that still works. Both are implemented as alternative
\texttt{ObjectiveConfig} presets, and Section~\ref{sec:exp:objectives} shows
that switching between all three presets requires no change at all to the
search algorithms of Chapter~\ref{chap:algorithms}, only to which quantity is
scored and which are checked for feasibility.

\section{Scope and Assumptions}
\label{sec:model:scope}

% Source: Optimizer Status.md §Known Limits

Three modelling choices bound what this thesis can claim, and it is worth
being explicit about them before presenting any algorithm or result.

First, only \emph{non-adaptive} schedules are represented: $T$ is fixed before
execution starts and cannot branch on an intermediate measurement outcome.
A fully adaptive scheme, where the choice of the next purification round
depends on the fidelity estimate so far, would need a different formulation,
most naturally a Markov decision process. \TOCITE{standard reference for
MDP / sequential decision-making formulation, e.g. Puterman or Sutton and
Barto, not yet in references.bib}. This is left to future work
(Section~\ref{sec:conc:future}).

Second, $M_{\max}$ is part of the budget $B$ but is not enforced anywhere in
the current implementation, neither during search nor during DAG validation.
Every reported resource cost $C(\Sigma)$ is therefore a lower bound on the
number of physical qubit slots a real device would need to run that schedule,
since it does not account for how many branches must be held in memory at
once.

Third, the error model throughout this thesis is the Pauli channel, the same
assumption made by the source paper. \TOCITE{general reference on Pauli
channels / stabilizer error models, e.g. Nielsen and Chuang, not yet in
references.bib}. Comparisons between schedules are made under this one noise
model, so a ranking that holds here is not automatically guaranteed to
transfer to a device dominated by a different error channel, for example pure
photon loss without any depolarizing component. This point is returned to in
Section~\ref{sec:disc:limitations}.
