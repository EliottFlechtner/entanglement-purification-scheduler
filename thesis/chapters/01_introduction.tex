\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:motivation}

Realizing a Quantum Internet~\cite{VanMeter2014Quantum,Kozlowski2023Architectural}, i.e. a network connecting quantum devices over long distances, would enable applications with no classical equivalent~\cite{Azuma2023Quantum,Hajdusek2023Quantum}. \Gls{qkd} provides a shared secret key whose secrecy is guaranteed by the laws of physics rather than by an eavesdropper's computing power~\cite{Bennett1992Quantum,Bennett2014Quantum,Scarani2009Security}. \Gls{dqc} lets several small quantum processors, connected by such a network, act together as one larger one~\cite{Cirac1999Distributed,Boschero2025Distributed}. Finally, networked quantum sensing improves measurement precision beyond what independent classically connected sensors can reach~\cite{Azuma2023Quantum,Proctor2017Networked,Proctor2018Multiparameter}. What each of these applications need from the network is the same underlying resource: entanglement, distributed between distant nodes as shared high-fidelity Bell pairs, also called \emph{ebits}. Direct photon transmission cannot supply it over long distances: channel loss grows exponentially with length, so the probability of a photon surviving the trip becomes negligible well before metropolitan-scale distances are reached. Quantum repeaters address this~\cite{Briegel1998Quantum} and Chapter~\ref{chap:related} reviews how successive repeater generations do so.

Regardless of what repeater generation is used, the delivered entanglement is noisy. Entanglement purification is one of the standard tools for turning that noisy entanglement into a usable resource. It consumes several independently generated copies of a shared pair and returns fewer copies of higher fidelity~\cite{Bennett1996Purification,Deutsch1996Quantum}. Each purification round spends copies and generation effort for a fidelity gain, while succeeding only probabilistically. In a multi-hop repeater chain, purification can be applied at many points at once: during generation, at intermediate stations, or at the two end nodes. Each application can be made in varying amounts and with different circuit choices. This thesis studies one practically motivated instance: the all-photonic repeater architecture built from \Glspl{rgs}~\cite{Azuma2015All}, in which purification can be applied directly during generation and swapping, rather than only after entanglement is already established. Deciding \emph{how} to allocate a fixed purification budget across such a chain so that the resulting rate or fidelity is as good as possible is a combinatorial scheduling problem and the central one this thesis addresses.

\section{The Scheduling Gap}
\label{sec:intro:gap}

Two recent papers, by Benchasattabuse, Hajdušek, and Van Meter, set up the specific architecture and the specific scheduling flexibility this thesis builds on. Throughout this thesis, \cite{Benchasattabuse2026Bridging} is referred to as \emph{the Bridging paper} and \cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} as \emph{the Integrating paper}; every later chapter uses these two shorthand names instead of repeating the full citation or author list. They might also be referred to collectively as \emph{the (two) source paper(s)}.

The Bridging paper introduces the \Gls{hrgs} building block: half of a \Gls{rgs}, generated and held at a single station rather than requiring the full state to exist in one place, allowing an end node to act as its own source of entanglement. This enables the all-photonic scheme to interoperate with conventional memory-equipped repeater nodes instead of requiring every intermediate station to be purely photonic~\cite{Benchasattabuse2026Bridging}. The Integrating paper then shows that entanglement purification can be inserted into this architecture at any stage, generation, link, or end node, and mixed freely between stages, provided it is executed \emph{optimistically}~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating,Mobayenjarihani2023Optimistic}, i.e. without waiting for a classical heralding outcome between rounds. Chapter~\ref{chap:background} formalises why this timing model is what makes purification worth inserting at all.

Both papers, however, demonstrate this flexibility with a single hand-chosen schedule, and the Integrating paper explicitly identifies searching for a better one, given a fixed physical network and resource budget, as an open problem. No systematic account exists of which allocation of a fixed budget across a chain actually performs best, or whether that answer changes with the noise model or the chain length. This is the scheduling gap this thesis addresses. Concretely, the research problem is: given a fixed network configuration (topology, per-hop noise parameters, resource budget), find a purification schedule from the combinatorially many ways of choosing where to purify, how many copies to consume, with which circuit, and in what order, that optimizes a stated objective such as rate, fidelity, or cost. The working hypothesis is that systematically searching this space yields schedules that are both measurably better than the hand-picked baseline at matched cost and, more importantly, feasible in regimes where that baseline is not. This thesis tests that hypothesis across several network configurations the source papers never considered, rather than only at their own single nominal configuration.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro:outline}

The remaining chapters proceed as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:related} surveys quantum repeater protocols, entanglement purification, and repeater-graph-state architectures, positioning this thesis relative to that state of the art. Chapter~\ref{chap:background} introduces the physical objects the rest of the thesis builds on: Bell pairs and the \Gls{hrgs} architecture, the error-vector and side-effect representations used to track a resource's quality through a schedule, and the purification circuits and optimistic-execution model from the Bridging and Integrating papers. Chapter~\ref{chap:model} turns these objects into a formal and searchable model: the network configuration, the state carried by each partially built resource, the schedule itself as a \Gls{dag} of operations, the resource budget, and the optimization objectives this thesis considers. Chapter~\ref{chap:algorithms} describes the shared evaluator and the three search tiers built on top of that model. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reports the experimental setup, validates the evaluator against the source papers, and presents the search results across the tested regimes. Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} draws out the five design findings, discusses when scheduling matters and when it does not, and states the scope within which the reported results should be read as optimal and the limitations of this work. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarises the contributions and outlines directions for future work.
