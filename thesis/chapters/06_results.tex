\paragraph{Baselines}
\label{sec:exp:baselines}

Three fixed non-adaptive baselines, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}, are used throughout: the \emph{raw chain}, with no purification; the \emph{uniform link-level baseline}, which applies the same copy count and circuit sequence at every hop; and the \emph{paper schedule}, the source paper's hand-picked Fig.\,4 example~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating}. As stated earlier, the latter is a demonstration of scheduling flexibility rather than an optimized design. The uniform link-level baseline and the paper schedule therefore represent distinct fixed strategies and are labelled separately below. The difference becomes particularly relevant under non-ideal inner-qubit noise, where the paper schedule can become infeasible while the uniform baseline remains feasible.

\paragraph{Metrics}
\label{sec:exp:metrics}

Fidelity $F$ is the Bell-pair fidelity after all purification, backbone, and correction operations, i.e.\ the $w$ component of the error vector at the schedule root (Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}). Rate is $R=P_{\mathrm{success}}/T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$, where $P_{\mathrm{success}}$ is the probability that a complete schedule attempt succeeds and $T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$ is its deterministic wall-clock duration. Resource cost $C$ counts the number of \Gls{hrgs} copies, or equivalently \texttt{GenNode} leaves, consumed per trial. Two comparison modes recur throughout this chapter: \emph{matched-cost}, in which the optimizer is constrained to $C=100$, and \emph{budget-relaxed}, in which it may choose any schedule satisfying $C\le E_{\max}$.

The evaluator of Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator} reproduces the source paper's Fig.\,5~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} to four significant figures. \section{Optimizer versus the Paper Schedule}
\label{sec:exp:comparison}

This section first compares the search results with the source paper's schedule at the reference configuration, then examines the effect of non-ideal inner-qubit noise and outer-photon noise.

\subsection{Headline Comparison}
\label{sec:exp:headline}

% Source: outputs/headline_experiment_n10/README.md,
%          outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

At $N=10$ and $e_d=0.01$, the optimizer improves on the paper schedule at both matched cost and under a relaxed budget. Table~\ref{tab:headline} reports the four variants. At matched cost, the optimizer returns $F=0.9168$ and $R=4158.14$ at $C=100$, compared with $F=0.9295$ and $R=4055.92$ for the paper schedule, giving a $2.5\%$ rate improvement while remaining above the fidelity floor. With the budget relaxed and same-span pumping enabled (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}), the optimizer returns $C=60$, $F=0.9063$, and $R=6195.95$, using $60\%$ of the paper schedule's cost while increasing rate by $52.8\%$. With pumping disabled, the search instead returns $C=50$, $F=0.9047$, and $R=6713.18$. Both search settings are retained in the comparison because the pumping extension changes the beam-limited search space and is studied separately in Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}.

\subsection{Network Generalization}
\label{sec:exp:netgen}

% Source: outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

The headline comparison uses the source paper's idealized network configuration. Introducing non-zero inner-qubit error changes which schedules remain feasible. At the same generation cost, the paper schedule can fall below the $F\ge0.9$ threshold, while a search rerun under the changed physical parameters finds a different schedule that remains feasible. Table~\ref{tab:netgen} and Figure~\ref{fig:netgen} compare the paper schedule with the optimizer under two non-ideal configurations, distinguished by their \Gls{bsm} arm count, at $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=100$, and $\gamma=0$.

The main result is a feasibility difference rather than a rate improvement: the paper schedule is infeasible in both non-ideal configurations, while the searched schedules remain feasible at the same cost. Raw rate comparisons are therefore not meaningful when the reference schedule fails the fidelity requirement.

The tree branching factor is not used as a sensitivity axis in this chapter because it does not affect $F$, $C$, or $R$ in the model of Chapter~\ref{chap:model}. Hop length is considered separately below; uniformly rescaling every hop length would multiply all schedule latencies by the same factor and leave relative schedule comparisons unchanged, whereas the heterogeneous hop lengths tested in Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous} change the relative physical conditions between hops.

\subsection{Per-Hop Heterogeneous Networks}
\label{sec:exp:heterogeneous}

% Source: outputs/random_network_adaptation/{30 seeds,100 seeds}/README.md,
%         src/hrgs_scheduler/models/random_network.py

Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} changes the noise model while keeping it uniform across hops. A real-world deployment, by contrast, can have different link lengths, hardware quality, and error rates from one hop to another. This section tests that setting by assigning each hop's length, inner-qubit error rates, and arm count independently at random. The search remains non-adaptive: the schedule is fixed once selected, and only the network configuration supplied to the search changes.

A five-hop network with one hop assigned a $12\times$ higher inner-qubit error rate than its neighbours provides a concrete test. At $E_{\max}=50$ and $F_{\min}=0.9$, the best uniform link-level candidate has $C=40$ and $F=0.88$ and is infeasible, while the optimizer finds a schedule with $C=38$, $F=0.90$, and $R=1798.57$ that clears the threshold by allocating resources differently across the hops.

\input{tables/weak_link_contrast.tex}

The same tendency is observed over 100 independently generated random $N=6$ networks (seeds 0--99, $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=60$, $F_{\min}=0.9$). Among the networks for which a feasible uniform link-level candidate exists, the optimizer improves rate by a mean of $20.37\%$ and never performs worse in the tested sample. Further interpretation of this behaviour is deferred to Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.

\subsection{Noise Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:edsweep}

% Source: outputs/sweep_ed_n10/README.md

Sweeping $e_d$ from 0 to 0.01 at $N=10$ shows that the optimizer's rate advantage over the uniform link-level baseline increases with noise, from near zero at $e_d=0$ to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$. Figure~\ref{fig:edsweep} shows the corresponding rate and fidelity curves for the paper baseline, the matched-cost optimizer, and the budget-relaxed optimizer. At low noise, the three variants remain close; the rate curves begin to separate visibly around $e_d=0.004$. The matched-cost optimizer may also change schedule structure as the noise level varies, so its curve should be interpreted as the best cost-$100$ result at each point rather than as one fixed schedule followed throughout the sweep. \paragraph{Alternative objectives}
\label{sec:exp:objectives}

Changing only the optimization objective, from maximizing rate to maximizing fidelity or minimizing cost, produces different top-ranked schedules without changing the search algorithm. Maximizing fidelity subject to the paper's rate floor reaches $F=0.94$ at $C=80$, compared with $F=0.91$ at $C=60$ for the rate-maximizing objective. Minimizing cost subject only to $F\ge0.9$ returns $C=60$ but a rate as low as $R=1239.19$; adding an explicit rate floor restores $R=6195.95$ at the same cost. Appendix~\ref{app:objectives} gives the complete five-objective comparison.

\section{Scaling with Chain Length}
\label{sec:exp:scaling}

\subsection{Budget Scaling}
\label{sec:exp:budget}

% Source: outputs/sweep_min_budget_vs_n/README.md

For $N$ ranging from 10 to 28, the minimum $E_{\max}$ for which a schedule within the searched families reaches $F_{\min}=0.9$ is found by bisection, rerunning the beam search at each trial budget. A descriptive power-law fit to the resulting points in log-log space gives
\[
    E_{\max}^{\min} \approx 1.192\,N^{1.666},
\]
with an exponent greater than one over the measured range. The paper's budget is first exceeded at $N=28$, where the minimum feasible budget is $E_{\max}=283$, compared with $10\times28=280$; at $N=26$, the minimum is $258$, compared with $10\times26=260$. The observed crossover therefore lies between $N=26$ and $N=28$. Figure~\ref{fig:budget} shows the measured points together with the paper's linear formula and the fitted power law.

These fitted values describe the minimum budget found within the searched schedule families at the chosen beam width, not a global lower bound over the full legal schedule space or an asymptotic result (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}). Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} discusses this scope limitation together with the excluded-construction result of Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}.

Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} additionally reports the minimum feasible budget as a function of $e_d$ at fixed $N=10$.

\section{Timing Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:timing}

% Source: outputs/sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

Memory dephasing and generation latency affect schedules through their timing structure, including the placement of \texttt{HeraldNode}s and \texttt{IdleNode}s (Section~\ref{sec:model:dag}), rather than through aggregate fidelity or resource cost alone. Sweeping the memory dephasing rate $\gamma$ leaves the raw chain and paper schedule unchanged over the tested range, while heralded end-node pumping falls toward the maximally mixed state as $\gamma$ increases because its sacrificial copies remain in memory during the round-trip heralding delays. The schedules selected by the optimizer remain flat because the search favours optimistic schedules that avoid those intermediate waits. Sweeping the generation latency $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$ reduces the rate of every schedule; the detailed curves are given in Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps}. Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} interprets these timing effects in the broader context of the scheduling problem.
