\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\paragraph{Parameters and sweeps}
\label{sec:exp:params}

Unless stated otherwise, all experiments use the beam search with $w_{\text{beam}}=25$ (Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam}) together with the fixed schedule families of Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}. They start from the reference configuration $\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:model:network}), with $N=10$, $e_d=0.01$, $F_{\min}=0.9$, and $E_{\max}=100$. Each experiment changes only the parameters specified for that experiment; all remaining parameters are held fixed. A complete summary of the experimental parameters and sweep ranges is provided in Table~\ref{tab:params} in Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps}.

The tree branching factor is not varied because it does not affect $F$, $C$, or $R$ in the model of Chapter~\ref{chap:model}. Uniform hop-length scaling likewise leaves relative schedule comparisons unchanged; heterogeneous hop lengths are considered separately in Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous}.

\paragraph{Baselines}
\label{sec:exp:baselines}

Three fixed non-adaptive baselines, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}, are used throughout: the \emph{raw chain}, with no purification; the \emph{uniform link-level baseline}, which applies the same copy count and circuit sequence at every hop; and the \emph{paper schedule}, the source paper's hand-picked Fig.\,4 example~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating}. The paper schedule is a demonstration of scheduling flexibility rather than an optimized design. The uniform link-level baseline and the paper schedule therefore represent distinct fixed strategies and are labelled separately throughout the experiments.

\paragraph{Metrics}
\label{sec:exp:metrics}

Fidelity $F$ is the Bell-pair fidelity after all purification, backbone, and correction operations, i.e.\ the $w$ component of the error vector at the schedule root (Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}). Rate is $R=P_{\mathrm{success}}/T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$, where $P_{\mathrm{success}}$ is the probability that a complete schedule attempt succeeds and $T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$ is its deterministic wall-clock duration. Resource cost $C$ counts the number of \Gls{hrgs} copies, or equivalently \texttt{GenNode} leaves, consumed per trial. Two comparison modes recur throughout this chapter: \emph{matched-cost}, in which the optimizer is constrained to $C=100$, and \emph{budget-relaxed}, in which it may choose any schedule satisfying $C\le E_{\max}$.

\section{Physics Validation}
\label{sec:exp:validation}

% Source: experiments/fig5_fidelity_vs_noise.py,

The evaluator of Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator} reproduces the source paper's Fig.\,5~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} to four significant figures. At $e_d=0.01$, the raw chain, heralded end-node pumping, and optimistic end-node pumping give fidelities consistent with the corresponding values reported by the paper. This agreement validates the physical model used by the search tiers, but does not establish search optimality. Appendix~\ref{app:validation} gives the corresponding comparison across the swept range of $e_d$.

\subsection{Per-Hop Heterogeneous Networks}
\label{sec:exp:heterogeneous}

% Source: outputs/random_network_adaptation/{30 seeds,100 seeds}/README.md,
%         src/hrgs_scheduler/models/random_network.py

Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} changes the noise model while keeping it uniform across hops. A real-world deployment, by contrast, can have different link lengths, hardware quality, and error rates from one hop to another. This section tests that setting by assigning each hop's length, inner-qubit error rates, and arm count independently at random. The search remains non-adaptive: the schedule is fixed once selected, and only the network configuration supplied to the search changes.

A five-hop network with one hop assigned a $12\times$ higher inner-qubit error rate than its neighbours provides a concrete example of the benefit of allocating purification resources unevenly across a heterogeneous chain. The detailed results are provided in Table~\ref{tab:weaklink} in Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps}.

The same tendency is observed over 100 independently generated random $N=6$ networks (seeds 0--99, $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=60$, $F_{\min}=0.9$). Among the networks for which a feasible uniform link-level candidate exists, the optimizer improves rate by a mean of $20.37\%$ and does not perform worse in the tested sample. Further interpretation of this behaviour is deferred to Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}.

\subsection{Noise Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:edsweep}

% Source: outputs/sweep_ed_n10/README.md

Sweeping $e_d$ from 0 to 0.01 at $N=10$ shows that the optimizer's rate advantage over the uniform link-level baseline increases with noise. Figure~\ref{fig:edsweep} shows the corresponding rate and fidelity curves for the paper baseline, the matched-cost optimizer, and the budget-relaxed optimizer. At low noise, the three variants remain close; the rate curves begin to separate visibly around $e_d=0.004$. \paragraph{Alternative objectives}
\label{sec:exp:objectives}

Changing the optimization objective from maximizing rate to maximizing fidelity or minimizing cost produces different top-ranked schedules without changing the search algorithm. Maximizing fidelity subject to a rate floor returns a higher-fidelity schedule than the rate-maximizing objective, while minimizing cost with only a fidelity floor can select a substantially lower-rate schedule. Adding an explicit rate floor changes that selection without modifying the search procedure. The complete numerical comparison is given in Appendix~\ref{app:objectives}.

\section{Scaling with Chain Length}
\label{sec:exp:scaling}

\subsection{Budget Scaling}
\label{sec:exp:budget}

% Source: outputs/sweep_min_budget_vs_n/README.md

For $N$ ranging from 10 to 28, the minimum $E_{\max}$ for which a schedule within the searched families reaches $F_{\min}=0.9$ is found by bisection, rerunning the beam search at each trial budget. A descriptive power-law fit to the resulting points in log-log space gives
\[
    E_{\max}^{\min} \approx 1.192\,N^{1.666},
\]
with an exponent greater than one over the measured range. The paper's budget is first exceeded at $N=28$, while remaining sufficient at $N=26$. Figure~\ref{fig:budget} shows the measured points together with the paper's linear budget and the fitted power law.

These fitted values describe the minimum budget found within the searched schedule families at the chosen beam width, not a global lower bound over the full legal schedule space or an asymptotic result (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}). Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} discusses this scope limitation together with the excluded-construction result of Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}.

Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} additionally reports the minimum feasible budget as a function of $e_d$ at fixed $N=10$.

\section{Timing Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:timing}

% Source: outputs/sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

Memory dephasing and generation latency affect schedules through their timing structure, including the placement of \texttt{HeraldNode}s and \texttt{IdleNode}s (Section~\ref{sec:model:dag}). Sweeping the memory dephasing rate $\gamma$ leaves the raw chain and paper schedule unchanged over the tested range, while heralded end-node pumping degrades as $\gamma$ increases because its sacrificial copies remain in memory during the round-trip heralding delays. The schedules selected by the optimizer remain flat over the tested range because the search favours optimistic schedules. Sweeping the generation latency $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$ reduces the rate of every schedule. Detailed timing curves and the corresponding parameter sweeps are given in Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps}.
