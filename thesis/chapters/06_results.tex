\paragraph{Baselines}
\label{sec:exp:baselines}

\TOFIX{commas make the reading unclear}{Three baselines are used throughout, all fixed, non-adaptive schedules already} introduced in Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}: the \emph{raw chain} (no purification at any stage), the \emph{uniform link-level baseline} (the same copy count and circuit sequence applied at every hop), and the \emph{paper schedule}, the source paper's own hand-picked Fig.\,4 example~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating}, which its authors present as one demonstration of scheduling flexibility rather than an optimized design.

The uniform link-level baseline and the paper schedule are not the same strategy, and are labelled separately in the figures and tables below. The paper schedule mixes three structurally different branches, a fully link-purified chain, two half-chain segments each purified internally, and one raw chain, combined at the end nodes (Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}). The uniform link-level baseline instead repeats one identical circuit at every hop, with no such mixing. Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} shows that this structural difference matters: under non-ideal inner-qubit noise, the paper schedule fails the fidelity threshold in configurations where the uniform link-level baseline remains feasible.

\paragraph{Metrics}
\label{sec:exp:metrics}

Fidelity $F$ is the Bell-pair fidelity after all purification, backbone, and correction operations, i.e.\ the $w$ component of the error vector at the schedule's root (Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}). Rate is $R=P_{\mathrm{success}}/T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$, where $P_{\mathrm{success}}$ is the probability that a complete schedule attempt succeeds and $T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$ is its deterministic wall-clock duration. Resource cost $C$ counts the number of \Gls{hrgs} copies (\texttt{GenNode} leaves) consumed per trial \TOFIX{weird to specify here, clear without probably}{; it is only the generation-cost component of the full budget $B=(n_{\mathrm{pur}},E_{\max},M_{\max})$ (Section~\ref{sec:model:budget})}. \TOFIX{good but to put earlier? also entire paragraph restates a lot of stuff said in previous chapters but for completeness of experimental setup to keep here}{Two comparison modes recur throughout this chapter: \emph{matched-cost}, in which the optimizer is constrained to $C=100$ (the paper schedule's own cost at the reference configuration), and \emph{budget-relaxed}, in which it may choose any schedule satisfying $C\le E_{\max}$. Both modes share the same $E_{\max}$, $n_{\mathrm{pur}}$, and $M_{\max}$; only the returned schedule's own cost $C$ differs between them.}

\subsection{Network Generalization}
\label{sec:exp:netgen}

% Source: outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

The headline comparison used the source paper's own idealized network configuration (Section~\ref{sec:model:network}). Introducing non-zero inner-qubit error, a form of noise the source paper does not model, changes which schedule actually \TOFIX{what does work mean here? change word to is feasible or something}{works}. At the same generation cost, the paper schedule can fall below the $F\ge0.9$ threshold entirely, while the search, re-run against the changed physics, finds a different and non-uniform schedule, which spends its cost differently across hops rather than repeating one fixed circuit, that still clears it. This is the strongest result in this chapter: a case where the fixed hand-designed schedule stops working entirely, while a schedule found by search \TOFIX{same remark about "work" being used to mean feasible}{continues to work}.

Table~\ref{tab:netgen} and Figure~\ref{fig:netgen} compare the paper schedule with the optimizer under two non-idealized configurations, distinguished by their \Gls{bsm} arm count (18 or 6), at $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=100$, and $\gamma=0$; the fully idealized reference configuration was already given in Table~\ref{tab:headline}, where every schedule is feasible.

The main result here is a difference in feasibility (a schedule is \emph{feasible} if it clears the fidelity floor $F\ge F_{\min}$, Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}), not the rate improvement reported in Table~\ref{tab:netgen}: the paper schedule is infeasible in both non-idealized configurations, while the searched schedules remain feasible at the same cost. An infeasible schedule's raw rate is not a meaningful comparison point, regardless of its value. The point is structural: a fixed, hand-designed schedule can silently stop working once the physical assumptions it was built for change, while a schedule constructed by search against the actual physics does not.

\subsection{Per-Hop Heterogeneous Networks}
\label{sec:exp:heterogeneous}

% Source: outputs/random_network_adaptation/{30 seeds,100 seeds}/README.md,
%         src/hrgs_scheduler/models/random_network.py

Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} changed the noise model but kept it uniform across hops. A \TOFIX{clarify more to mean that deployed means "deployed in real life settings"}{deployed} network, by contrast, has station spacing and hardware quality that vary hop to hop. This section tests that case directly, by assigning each hop's length, inner-qubit error rates, and arm count independently at random, and asks whether a schedule found by search adapts to a network it was never specifically designed for. No adaptive or closed-loop mechanism is involved: the schedule returned by the search is still fixed once chosen (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}); only which fixed schedule the search selects depends on the given network.

A five-hop network with one hop set to a $12\times$ higher inner-qubit error rate than its neighbours illustrates the effect concretely: at $E_{\max}=50$ and $F_{\min}=0.9$, no uniform link-level candidate clears the fidelity floor at any cost, while the schedule found by search clears it at a lower cost, by spending more generation nodes on the weak hop and fewer elsewhere (Table~\ref{tab:weaklink}). A uniform strategy is not merely suboptimal on this network: it is infeasible at every cost considered, while a schedule shaped differently per hop restores feasibility at lower cost.

This single designed case is confirmed on average over a sample of 100 independently random $N=6$ networks (seeds 0--99, $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=60$, $F_{\min}=0.9$): among the networks with a feasible uniform link-level candidate, the optimizer's best feasible schedule improves rate by a mean of $20.37\%$ over that candidate, and never does worse. \subsection{Noise Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:edsweep}

% Source: outputs/sweep_ed_n10/README.md

Sweeping $e_d$ from 0 to 0.01 at $N=10$ shows that the optimizer's rate advantage over the uniform link-level baseline grows with noise, from near zero at $e_d=0$ to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$. Figure~\ref{fig:edsweep} plots rate and fidelity against $e_d$ for the three variants of Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline}.

At low noise, the advantage is small because the fidelity floor is easy to satisfy and many schedules perform similarly (Section~\ref{sec:disc:nearoptimal}); the rate curves separate visibly only once $e_d$ exceeds about $0.004$. The fidelity panel shows the paper schedule's curve consistently above the matched-cost optimizer's; this is expected rather than a discrepancy, since the matched-cost objective maximizes rate subject to $F\ge0.9$, not fidelity itself, so it spends the same cost on a schedule that clears the floor with less margin and a higher rate instead. The matched-cost fidelity curve is also not perfectly smooth: it jumps upward near $e_d=0.004$ because the best-ranked cost-$100$ schedule switches circuit family there (from a $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{YY}}$-only to a $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{XZ}}$-only sequence), and matched-cost always reports whichever cost-$100$ schedule currently ranks best, not a fixed schedule shape followed across the sweep. The budget-relaxed optimizer remains closest to the maximum rate observed at each noise level over most of the sweep, since it can spend less at low noise and reserve the freed cost for extra purification as noise increases.

\paragraph{Alternative objectives}
\label{sec:exp:objectives}
Changing only the optimization objective, maximizing fidelity subject to a rate floor, or minimizing cost subject to a fidelity floor, produces structurally different top-ranked schedules without changing the search algorithm itself. At $N=10$, $e_d=0.01$: maximizing fidelity under the paper's own rate floor reaches $F=0.94$ at $C=80$ (vs.\ $F=0.91$ at $C=60$ when maximizing rate); minimizing cost under only a fidelity floor returns $C=60$ but a rate as low as $R=1239.19$, since nothing in that objective prefers a higher-rate schedule at the same cost, unless a rate floor is also imposed, which restores $R=6195.95$ at the same $C=60$. Constraints must therefore reflect every performance requirement that matters to the intended use, not only the primary objective. Appendix~\ref{app:objectives} gives the full five-objective comparison.

\subsection{Budget Scaling}
\label{sec:exp:budget}

% Source: outputs/sweep_min_budget_vs_n/README.md

For $N$ ranging from 10 to 28, the minimum $E_{\max}$ for which a schedule within the searched families reaches $F_{\min}=0.9$ is found by bisection: repeatedly halving the search interval on $E_{\max}$ and re-running the beam search at each trial value until the smallest feasible budget is pinned down. A descriptive power-law fit to the resulting points in log-log space gives
\[
    E_{\max}^{\min} \approx 1.192\,N^{1.666},
\]
with an exponent greater than one, indicating that the required budget grows faster over the measured range than the source paper's linear $10N$ budget formula. The paper's budget is first exceeded at $N=28$, where the minimum feasible budget is $E_{\max}=283$, just above $10\times28=280$; at $N=26$ the minimum feasible budget is $258$, just under $10\times26=260$. The crossover therefore lies between $N=26$ and $N=28$. Figure~\ref{fig:budget} plots these measured points against the paper's linear formula and this fitted power law.

These fitted values describe the minimum budget found within the searched schedule families under this beam width, not a global lower bound over the full legal schedule space (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}) nor an asymptotic result; Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} discusses this scope limitation in full, together with the excluded-construction result of Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded} (Appendix~\ref{app:results}).

Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} additionally shows, by bisecting the minimum feasible budget over $e_d$ instead of $N$ at fixed $N=10$, that the paper's own fixed $E_{\max}=100$ overspends by $2.0$--$4.8\times$ across the tested noise range -- a stronger, self-contained claim than the $N$-scaling crossover above, since it needs none of that crossover's beam-width caveats.

\section{Timing Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:timing}

% Source: outputs/sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

Memory dephasing and generation latency both affect a schedule according to its timing structure (where its \texttt{HeraldNode}s and \texttt{IdleNode}s sit, Section~\ref{sec:model:dag}), not its fidelity or resource cost. Sweeping the memory dephasing rate $\gamma$ leaves schedules with no waiting branch (the raw chain, the paper schedule) unaffected, while heralded end-node pumping collapses toward the maximally mixed state as $\gamma$ grows, since its sacrificial copies sit in memory across each round-trip heralding delay; the schedules selected by the optimizer stay flat because the search favours optimistic schedules that defer those delays (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}). Sweeping the generation latency $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$ instead reduces every schedule's rate monotonically, more so for schedules with a smaller fixed latency to begin with. Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} gives both sweeps in full, with figures.
