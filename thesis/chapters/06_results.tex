\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:exp:baselines}

Three baselines are used throughout.
The \emph{raw chain} applies no purification.
The \emph{uniform link-level baseline} applies the same copy count and circuit
sequence at every hop, representing the practitioner's natural default.
The \emph{paper schedule} is the hand-chosen schedule from the source
paper's Fig.\,4 (Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}), acknowledged by its
authors as unoptimised.
The uniform link-level baseline and the paper schedule are distinct and must
be labelled separately in figures: Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} shows that
the paper schedule can fail the fidelity floor entirely under non-ideal
physics while the uniform link-level baseline does not.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:exp:metrics}

Fidelity $F$ is the Bell-pair fidelity after all purification and correction
steps. Rate $R = P_{\mathrm{success}} / T_{\mathrm{gen}}$ combines success
probability and generation time. Resource cost $C$ counts \Gls{hrgs} copies
consumed per trial. Two comparison modes are used: \emph{matched-cost}
(optimizer constrained to $C=100$) and \emph{budget-relaxed} (optimizer free
within $C \le E_{\max}$).

\section{Physics Validation}
\label{sec:exp:validation}

% Source: experiments/fig5_fidelity_vs_noise.py,
%          outputs/headline_experiment_n10/README.md

The evaluator (Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator}) reproduces the source paper's
Fig.\,5 to four significant figures. At $e_d=0.01$, the raw, heralded-pumping, and
optimistic-pumping canonical schedules yield $F \in \{0.8234, 0.9168, 0.9295\}$ against the
paper's $\approx\{0.823, 0.917, 0.929\}$. This agreement confirms the physical
model. It is not a claim about the search quality.
Appendix~\ref{app:validation} gives the full evaluator-vs.-analytical
comparison across several $e_d$ values.

\section{Headline Comparison}
\label{sec:exp:headline}

% Source: outputs/headline_experiment_n10/README.md,
%          outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

At $N=10$, $e_d=0.01$, the paper schedule has
$C=100$, $F=0.9295$, $R=4055.92$.
The optimizer at matched cost ($C=100$) returns $F=0.9168$, $R=4158.14$
(+2.5\%), trading surplus fidelity for higher success probability.
The budget-relaxed optimizer, searched with the same-span pumping move
enabled (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}), returns $C=60$,
$F=0.9063$, $R=6195.95$: it spends 60\% of the paper's budget and achieves
52.8\% higher rate.
The same search with the pumping move disabled instead returns $C=50$,
$F=0.9047$, $R=6713.18$: this predates the pumping extension and is kept
here as the historical headline number.
All four variants are labelled separately in Table~\ref{tab:headline}.

\section{Noise Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:edsweep}

% Source: outputs/sweep_ed_n10/README.md

Sweeping $e_d$ from 0 to 0.01 at $N=10$ shows that the optimizer's rate
advantage over the uniform link-level baseline grows with noise level,
reaching up to 18.2\% at $e_d=0.01$.
Figure~\ref{fig:edsweep} plots rate and fidelity against $e_d$ for the
three schedule variants of Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline}: the paper
baseline, the optimizer at matched cost, and the optimizer under a
relaxed budget.
At low noise the advantage is small: the fidelity floor is easy to clear and
most schedules are nearly equivalent, a regime discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:disc:nearoptimal}.
The rate curves only separate visibly once $e_d$ exceeds about $0.004$;
below that point all three variants sit within a few percent of the
noiseless maximum rate.
The budget-relaxed optimizer stays closest to that maximum for the
longest stretch of the sweep, since a lower fixed cost leaves more of its
budget available to add purification once noise starts to erode fidelity.

\subsection{Alternative Objectives}
\label{sec:exp:objectives}

% Source: outputs/alternative_objectives/README.md

Changing only the optimization objective, from maximizing rate to maximizing
fidelity or minimizing cost, returns structurally different top schedules
without any change to the search algorithm itself.
Table~\ref{tab:objectives} shows five objective definitions at $N=10$, $e_d=0.01$:
maximize rate ($C=60$, $F=0.9063$, $R=6195.95$);
maximize fidelity with paper-rate floor ($C=80$, $F=0.9351$, $R=4804.58$);
maximize fidelity with matched-rate floor ($C=80$, $F=0.9351$, $R=4804.58$);
minimize cost with $F \ge 0.9$ only ($C=60$, $R=1239.19$);
minimize cost with $F \ge 0.9$ and $R \ge 4055.92$ ($C=60$, $R=6195.95$).
The fourth definition can return a valid but low-rate schedule even
though a better-rate schedule at the same cost exists: a user of this
objective who also cares about rate must add an explicit $r_{\min}$ floor
of their own, as the fifth definition does.

\section{Network Generalization}
\label{sec:exp:netgen}

% Source: outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

When the inner-qubit error source is turned on (moving away from the paper's
idealized physics), the paper schedule fails the
$F \ge 0.9$ floor while the optimizer, given the same budget, finds a different
schedule shape that clears it.
Table~\ref{tab:netgen} shows results for three network configurations at
$e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=100$.

The headline finding is a feasibility difference, not a rate percentage:
the paper schedule is infeasible in two of the three configurations.
Raw rate comparisons between an infeasible baseline and a feasible optimizer
result are therefore not apples-to-apples for those configurations.
Branching and hop length were excluded as sensitivity axes: branching has no
effect on $F$, $C$, or $R$ in the model of Chapter~\ref{chap:model}, and
uniformly rescaling hop length rescales every schedule's latency by the same
constant factor, leaving all relative comparisons unchanged.

\section{Budget Scaling}
\label{sec:exp:budget}

% Source: outputs/sweep_min_budget_vs_n/README.md

For each $N \in \{10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26, 28\}$, the
minimum $E_{\max}$ at which any searched-family schedule clears $F_{\min}=0.9$
is found by bisection.
A descriptive power-law fit over the 12-point log-log data gives
$E_{\max}^{\min} \approx 1.369 \cdot N^{1.620}$, an exponent above 1, meaning
the minimum required budget grows faster than the paper's linear $10N$ formula.
The paper's budget is first exceeded at $N=28$: the minimum feasible
$E_{\max} = 283 > 10 \times 28 = 280$ (ratio 1.011).
At $N=26$ the ratio is still 0.992, so the crossover is bracketed between
$N=26$ and $N=28$.

Three required caveats: (i)~the fit is descriptive over 12 points, not a proven
asymptotic law; (ii)~the crossover is beam-width-sensitive and provisional;
(iii)~the excluded-move result at $N=18$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded})
shows that a schedule outside the searched families is feasible at the paper's
own budget, so these are lower bounds on the searched-family minimum, not true
global lower bounds.
Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} collects supplementary sweep figures (beam width,
$e_d$, timing/pumping) that support this discussion without interrupting it.

Fixing $N=10$ and bisecting over $e_d$ shows that the minimum feasible budget

\subsection{Optimality Bound at $N=18$}
\label{sec:exp:excluded}

% Source: outputs/excluded_move_n14_n18/README.md,
%          docs/Optimality Scope.md

A hand-constructed schedule using the same-span excluded move (purifying two
independently built $\mathrm{Span}(0,18)$ candidates) yields $F=0.928596$ at
$E_{\max}=180$, the paper's own budget for $N=18$.
The beam search itself cannot reach this schedule, since it lies outside the
families the search considers even with the pumping move enabled
(Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}).
This is an optimality-scope result, not a scalability claim: it shows that the
searched families do not cover the full legal schedule space, consistent with
the scope stated in Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}.

\section{Timing Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:timing}

% Source: outputs/sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

Both sensitivity axes explored here are specific to a schedule's execution
\emph{shape} rather than its aggregate fidelity or cost.

\paragraph{Memory dephasing ($\gamma$)}
Sweeping $\gamma$ from 0 to $10^5$ at $N=10$, $e_d=0.01$,
the raw chain and the paper schedule are bit-for-bit flat across
the entire range: neither ever combines two branches that became ready at
different simulated times, so the added decoherence is always zero.
Heralded end-node pumping collapses from $F=0.9168$ at $\gamma=0$
to $F=0.250$ (maximally mixed) at $\gamma=10^5$, because its sacrificial
copies wait in memory during each round-trip herald.
The schedules the optimizer actually selects are confirmed flat at every
tested $\gamma$: not because $\gamma$ is universally harmless, but because
the search already favours the optimistic pumping family, which never
exposes this failure mode (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}).

\paragraph{Generation latency ($\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$)}
Sweeping $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$ at $N=10$, $\gamma=0$, rate falls monotonically
for every schedule.
The relative loss is largest for the optimistic pumping family
($1 \times L/c$ baseline latency) and smallest for heralded end-node pumping
($9 \times L/c$), since a schedule that already pays a larger fixed latency
is proportionally less affected by an added per-photon delay.
The two schedules cross over near $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}} \approx 0.01$: above
that threshold, heralded pumping's larger fixed latency actually makes it the
less rate-sensitive of the two.
