\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{chap:results}

% Sources: outputs/*/README.md, experiments/*.py,
%          docs/Design Principles.md

This chapter first validates the physical evaluator against the source paper's fidelity results (Section~\ref{sec:exp:validation}), then compares the paper schedule with schedules found by search (Section~\ref{sec:exp:comparison}): a matched-cost/budget-relaxed check at the reference configuration, followed immediately by the chapter's central result, that under physics the source paper did not consider, the paper's fixed schedule can become infeasible while a re-run search finds a different, non-uniform schedule that still works, and by how that advantage changes with noise. Section~\ref{sec:exp:tradeoffs} then exposes the full cost-quality tradeoff the search has access to and how substituting the optimization objective reshapes it. Section~\ref{sec:exp:scaling} asks the same question along chain length, where the present search also reaches its clearest limits. A final section studies timing effects caused by memory dephasing and generation latency. The main-text figures and tables present the results needed to follow these comparisons; supplementary runtime and beam-width analyses are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps}.

\paragraph{Baselines}
\label{sec:exp:baselines}

Three baselines are used throughout. The \emph{raw chain} applies no purification. The \emph{uniform link-level baseline} applies the same copy count and circuit sequence at every hop, representing a simple fixed strategy that does not adapt purification to the location within the chain. The \emph{paper schedule} is the hand-chosen schedule from the source paper's Fig.\,4 (Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}), which the authors present as an unoptimised example.

The uniform link-level baseline and the paper schedule are distinct and are labelled separately in the figures and tables. Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} shows that they can behave differently under non-ideal inner-qubit noise, with the paper schedule failing the fidelity threshold in configurations where the uniform link-level baseline remains feasible.

\paragraph{Metrics}
\label{sec:exp:metrics}

Fidelity $F$ is the Bell-pair fidelity after all purification, backbone, and correction operations. Rate is $R=P_{\mathrm{success}}/T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$, where $P_{\mathrm{success}}$ is the probability that a complete schedule attempt succeeds and $T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$ is its deterministic wall-clock duration under the timing model of Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}. Resource cost $C$ counts the number of \Gls{hrgs} copies consumed per trial. Two comparison modes are used: \emph{matched-cost}, in which the optimizer is constrained to $C=100$, and \emph{budget-relaxed}, in which it may choose any schedule satisfying $C\le E_{\max}$.

\section{Physics Validation}
\label{sec:exp:validation}

% Source: experiments/fig5_fidelity_vs_noise.py,
%          outputs/headline_experiment_n10/README.md

The evaluator described in Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator} reproduces the source paper's Fig.\,5 to four significant figures. At $e_d=0.01$, the raw, heralded-pumping, and optimistic-pumping canonical schedules yield $F\in\{0.8234,0.9168,0.9295\}$, compared with the paper's reported values of approximately $\{0.823,0.917,0.929\}$. This agreement validates the physical evaluation model used by the search; it is not evidence that the search itself is optimal.

Appendix~\ref{app:validation} gives the full evaluator-versus-analytical comparison across several values of $e_d$.

\section{Optimizer versus the Paper Schedule}
\label{sec:exp:comparison}

This section compares the paper schedule against schedules found by search, first at the paper's own reference configuration, then under network physics the paper does not model, then across a sweep of outer-photon noise.

\subsection{Headline Comparison}
\label{sec:exp:headline}

% Source: outputs/headline_experiment_n10/README.md,
%          outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

At $N=10$ and $e_d=0.01$, the paper schedule uses $C=100$ and achieves $F=0.9295$ and $R=4055.92$. At the same cost, the optimizer returns $F=0.9168$ and $R=4158.14$, a $2.5\%$ rate improvement obtained by accepting a lower but still feasible fidelity. The budget-relaxed optimizer, with the same-span pumping move enabled (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}), returns $C=60$, $F=0.9063$, and $R=6195.95$: it uses $60\%$ of the paper schedule's budget while achieving a $52.8\%$ higher rate. With same-span pumping disabled, the search instead returns $C=50$, $F=0.9047$, and $R=6713.18$; this predates the pumping extension and is retained as a historical headline result rather than treated as the current default. All variants are labelled separately in Table~\ref{tab:headline}.

This matched-cost/budget-relaxed comparison is a first check, not the main claim of this chapter: the paper's own Fig.\,4 schedule is explicitly presented by its authors as one unoptimised example, so a systematic search improving on it is close to expected. What it establishes is that the search finds real, structurally valid, non-uniform schedules that behave as the physical model predicts -- the basis for the stronger claims that follow.

\subsection{Network Generalization}
\label{sec:exp:netgen}

% Source: outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

The headline comparison used the source paper's own idealized error model. Turning on inner-qubit errors, which the source paper does not model, changes which schedule actually works. At the same generation budget, the paper schedule can fall below the $F\ge0.9$ threshold entirely, while the search, re-run against the changed physics, finds a different, non-uniform schedule that still clears it. This is the strongest result in this chapter: not a percentage improvement, but a case where the fixed hand-designed schedule stops working and a schedule found by search does not.

Table~\ref{tab:netgen} and Figure~\ref{fig:netgen} compare the paper baseline with the optimizer under two non-ideal network configurations at $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=100$, and $\gamma=0$; the reference (ideal) configuration was already given in Table~\ref{tab:headline}, where both schedules are feasible.

The main result here is a difference in feasibility rather than a rate improvement: the paper schedule is infeasible in both non-ideal configurations, while the searched schedules remain above the fidelity threshold at the same budget. Comparing the raw rates of the infeasible paper schedule and a feasible optimizer schedule is therefore not an apples-to-apples performance comparison -- the point is that a fixed, hand-designed schedule can silently stop working once the physical assumptions it was built for change, while a schedule constructed by search against the actual physics does not.

Branching factor and hop length are not used as sensitivity axes in this chapter. Within the model of Chapter~\ref{chap:model}, changing the tree branching factors does not affect $F$, $C$, or $R$, while uniformly rescaling every hop length multiplies all schedule latencies by the same factor and therefore leaves relative schedule comparisons unchanged.

\subsection{Per-Hop Heterogeneous Networks}
\label{sec:exp:heterogeneous}

% Source: outputs/random_network_adaptation/{30 seeds,100 seeds}/README.md,
%         src/hrgs_scheduler/models/random_network.py

Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} changed the noise \emph{model} but kept it uniform across hops: every hop in a given configuration still shares the same inner-qubit error rate and arm count. A real deployment would instead have station spacing and hardware quality that vary hop to hop. This section tests that case directly, using \texttt{random\_network\_config} to draw each hop's length, inner-qubit error rates, and arm count independently, and asks whether a schedule found by search still adapts to a network it was never specifically designed for, without any in-flight or closed-loop mechanism: the schedule remains fixed in advance for the whole configuration (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}), and only which fixed schedule the search selects depends on the given network.

\paragraph{A designed contrast}
A five-hop network is built with one hop set to a substantially higher inner-qubit error rate than the rest ($p^X_{\mathrm{in}}=p^Z_{\mathrm{in}}=0.015$ at hop 2, versus $0.001$ at every other hop, $e_d=0.01$, 18 arms and $2\,\mathrm{km}$ length at every hop), giving that hop an end-to-end inner-qubit error rate roughly $12\times$ higher than its neighbours ($0.211$ versus $0.018$). At $E_{\max}=50$, $F_{\min}=0.9$, no uniform link-level candidate (the same purification circuit sequence applied identically at every hop, Section~\ref{sec:exp:baselines}) clears the fidelity floor at any enumerated cost; the best one reaches only $F=0.8821$ at $C=40$. The schedule found by beam search clears the floor at $F=0.9007$, using \emph{fewer} generation nodes ($C=38$) and a higher rate (Table~\ref{tab:weaklink}). A uniform convention is not merely suboptimal on this network; it is infeasible at every cost this search considered, while a schedule shaped differently at each hop restores feasibility at lower cost.

    \label{tab:weaklink}
    \small

\end{table}

Figure~\ref{fig:weaklink} shows the generation-node count the winning schedule spends at each hop. The uniform recipe spends the same count everywhere by construction; the optimizer's schedule is free to spend unevenly and does so, without being told which hop is worse.

\paragraph{A randomized sweep}
A single designed example does not establish that this behaviour is typical, so the same comparison is repeated over 100 independently random $N=6$ networks (\texttt{random\_network\_config}, seeds 0--99, $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=60$, $F_{\min}=0.9$). For every network with a feasible uniform link-level candidate, the optimizer's best feasible schedule improves rate by a mean of $20.37\%$ and a median of $23.01\%$ over that candidate, with a range of $0.00\%$ to $47.81\%$ across the sample; 80 of the 100 networks show a strictly positive improvement, and none show a negative one. Unlike the designed contrast above, no network in this particular random sample produced a case where the uniform recipe was infeasible outright: bounding the sampled ranges (Table~\ref{tab:params}) keeps most draws inside the uniform baseline's reach, and the designed contrast's larger, adversarially-placed noise gap is what pushes that example into outright infeasibility.

Figure~\ref{fig:hetsweep} plots each network's rate improvement against a coefficient-of-variation summary of its per-hop noise heterogeneity. The two are only weakly related (Pearson correlation $0.020$): the sign of the benefit is consistent throughout the sample, but its size does not track this particular heterogeneity measure. This is a genuine null result rather than a gap in the analysis: a simple summary statistic of "how uneven is this network" is not sufficient to predict how much a per-hop-aware schedule gains over a uniform one, at least at this sample size and network scale.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/results/random_sweep_improvement_vs_heterogeneity.png}
    \caption{Optimizer rate improvement over the uniform link-level baseline against per-hop noise heterogeneity, across 100 random $N=6$ networks. The scatter shows no clear trend with this heterogeneity measure.}
    \label{fig:hetsweep}
\end{figure}

Taken together, these two results extend Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen}'s feasibility finding from a small number of hand-chosen alternate configurations to a genuinely random, per-hop heterogeneous sample: the advantage of letting a schedule vary across hops is not an artefact of the two specific non-ideal configurations tested earlier, and can on occasion be the difference between a feasible and an infeasible schedule at the same budget, though this sample did not by itself produce that outcome.

\subsection{Noise Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:edsweep}

% Source: outputs/sweep_ed_n10/README.md

Sweeping $e_d$ from 0 to 0.01 at $N=10$ shows that the optimizer's rate advantage over the uniform link-level baseline grows with noise, reaching up to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$. Figure~\ref{fig:edsweep} plots rate and fidelity against $e_d$ for the three main variants considered in Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline}: the paper baseline, the optimizer at matched cost, and the budget-relaxed optimizer.

At low noise, the advantage is small because the fidelity floor is easy to satisfy and many schedules perform similarly, corresponding to the near-optimal regime discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc:nearoptimal}. The rate curves begin to separate visibly once $e_d$ exceeds approximately $0.004$; below this value, all three variants remain within a few percent of the maximum rate observed in the sweep. The budget-relaxed optimizer remains closest to that maximum over the largest portion of the sweep because it uses fewer copies at low noise and therefore retains budget that can be spent on additional purification as noise increases.

\paragraph{Alternative objectives}
\label{sec:exp:objectives}
Changing only the optimization objective -- maximizing fidelity subject to a rate floor, or minimizing cost subject to a fidelity floor -- produces structurally different top-ranked schedules without changing the search algorithm itself. At $N=10$, $e_d=0.01$: maximizing fidelity under the paper's own rate floor reaches $F=0.9351$ at $C=80$ (vs.\ $F=0.9063$ at $C=60$ when maximizing rate); minimizing cost under only a fidelity floor returns $C=60$ but a rate as low as $R=1239.19$, since nothing in that objective prefers a higher-rate schedule at the same cost, unless a rate floor is also imposed, which restores $R=6195.95$ at the same $C=60$. Constraints must therefore reflect every performance requirement that matters to the intended use, not only the primary objective. Appendix~\ref{app:objectives} gives the full five-objective comparison.

\section{Scaling with Chain Length}
\label{sec:exp:scaling}

\subsection{Budget Scaling}
\label{sec:exp:budget}

% Source: outputs/sweep_min_budget_vs_n/README.md

For each $N\in\{10,12,14,16,18,20,21,22,23,24,26,28\}$, bisection is used to find the minimum $E_{\max}$ for which a schedule within the searched families reaches $F_{\min}=0.9$. A descriptive power-law fit to the resulting 12 points in log-log space gives
\[
    E_{\max}^{\min} \approx 1.369\,N^{1.620},
\]
with an exponent greater than one, indicating that the required budget grows faster over the measured range than the source paper's linear $10N$ budget formula. The paper's budget is first exceeded at $N=28$, where the minimum feasible budget is $E_{\max}=283>10\times28=280$, corresponding to a ratio of 1.011. At $N=26$, the ratio remains 0.992, so the observed crossover lies between $N=26$ and $N=28$.

Three qualifications are important. First, the fitted power law is descriptive over 12 measured points and is not an asymptotic result. Second, the location of the crossover depends on the beam width and should therefore be regarded as provisional. Third, the excluded-move result at $N=18$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}) shows that a schedule outside the searched families can remain feasible at the paper's own budget. The fitted values therefore describe the minimum budget found within the searched schedule families, not a global lower bound over the full legal schedule space.

Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} collects supplementary sweeps over beam width, $e_d$, timing, and pumping that support this analysis without interrupting the main discussion. Among these, bisecting the minimum feasible budget over $e_d$ instead of $N$, at fixed $N=10$, shows that the paper's own fixed $E_{\max}=100$ overspends by $2.0$--$4.8\times$ across the tested noise range: the minimum budget needed to clear $F_{\min}=0.9$ ranges from $E_{\max}=21$ at low noise up to $E_{\max}=50$ at $e_d=0.01$. This is a stronger, self-contained claim than the $N$-scaling crossover above: it holds entirely within the paper's own tested configuration ($N=10$) and needs none of that crossover's beam-width caveats.

\subsection{Optimality Bound at $N=18$}
\label{sec:exp:excluded}

% Source: outputs/excluded_move_n14_n18/README.md,
%          docs/Optimality Scope.md

A hand-constructed schedule using the same-span move excluded from the search (purifying two independently constructed $\mathrm{Span}(0,18)$ resources) achieves $F=0.928596$ at $E_{\max}=180$, which is the paper's budget for $N=18$. The beam search cannot reach this schedule because the move lies outside the schedule families considered by the search, even when same-span pumping is enabled (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}).

This is an optimality-scope result rather than a scalability result: it demonstrates that the searched families do not cover the full legal schedule space, consistent with the scope stated in Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}. It also provides a concrete example of why the bounded-search results must not be interpreted as proofs of global optimality.

\section{Timing Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:timing}

% Source: outputs/sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

The two timing sensitivity axes studied here affect the execution of a schedule according to its shape rather than its aggregate fidelity or resource cost.

\paragraph{Memory dephasing ($\gamma$)}

Sweeping $\gamma$ from 0 to $10^5$ at $N=10$ and $e_d=0.01$ leaves the raw chain and paper schedule numerically unchanged across the tested range: neither contains a resource that waits for another branch to become ready, so the simulated execution introduces no additional memory-decoherence interval for these schedules. By contrast, heralded end-node pumping falls from $F=0.9168$ at $\gamma=0$ to $F=0.250$, the maximally mixed limit, at $\gamma=10^5$, because its sacrificial copies remain stored while each round-trip heralding delay is incurred.

The schedules selected by the optimizer remain flat across the tested values of $\gamma$. This does not mean that memory dephasing is generally harmless; rather, the search favours optimistic schedules in which the relevant intermediate heralding delays are deferred, avoiding the specific waiting pattern that causes the collapse of the heralded pumping schedule (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}).

\paragraph{Generation latency ($\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$)}

Sweeping $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$ at $N=10$ and $\gamma=0$ reduces the rate monotonically for every schedule. The relative rate loss is largest for the optimistic pumping family, whose baseline latency is approximately $1\times L/c$, and smallest for heralded end-node pumping, whose baseline latency is approximately $9\times L/c$. A schedule that already has a larger fixed latency is proportionally less affected by the same additional per-photon generation delay. The two schedules cross near $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}\approx0.01$; above this value, the larger fixed latency of heralded pumping makes it less sensitive, in relative terms, to further increases in generation latency.
