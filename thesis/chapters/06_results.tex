\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{chap:results}

% Sources: outputs/*/README.md, experiments/*.py,
%          docs/Design Principles.md

Almost every result in this chapter concerns the Integrating paper~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} specifically, since it is the source of both the Fig.\,5 fidelity benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:exp:validation}) and the Fig.\,4 hand-picked schedule (Section~\ref{sec:exp:baselines}) this chapter's comparisons are built around. To avoid ambiguity between the two source papers, this chapter uses \emph{the paper schedule} for that Fig.\,4 example throughout, and names the Bridging paper~\cite{Benchasattabuse2026Bridging} explicitly whenever it, rather than the Integrating paper, is the relevant source.

This chapter first validates the evaluator against the source paper's own fidelity benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:exp:validation}), since every later comparison depends on the physical model being correct. Section~\ref{sec:exp:comparison} then compares the paper schedule against schedules found by search: a matched-cost and budget-relaxed check at the reference configuration, followed by the chapter's central result, that under noise the source paper does not model, the paper schedule can become infeasible while a re-run search still finds a working schedule, and how that advantage changes with noise level and network structure. Section~\ref{sec:exp:tradeoffs} exposes the cost-quality tradeoff available to the search and how changing the optimization objective reshapes it. Section~\ref{sec:exp:scaling} asks the same feasibility question along chain length, where the search also reaches its clearest limits. A final section studies timing effects from memory dephasing and generation latency. Supplementary runtime and beam-width analyses are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} rather than the main text.

\paragraph{Baselines}
\label{sec:exp:baselines}

Three baselines are used throughout. The \emph{raw chain} applies no purification at any stage. The \emph{uniform link-level baseline} applies the same copy count and circuit sequence at every hop, representing a simple fixed strategy that does not adapt purification to location within the chain. The \emph{paper schedule} is the hand-picked schedule from the source paper's Fig.\,4~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} (Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}): its own authors present it as one demonstration of scheduling flexibility, not as an optimized design.

The uniform link-level baseline and the paper schedule are both fixed, non-adaptive strategies, but they are not the same fixed strategy, and are labelled separately in the figures and tables. The paper schedule mixes three structurally different branches, a fully link-purified chain, two half-chain segments each purified internally, and one raw chain, combined at the end nodes (Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}). The uniform link-level baseline instead repeats one identical circuit at every hop, with no such mixing. Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} shows that this structural difference matters: under non-ideal inner-qubit noise, the paper schedule fails the fidelity threshold in configurations where the uniform link-level baseline remains feasible.

\paragraph{Metrics}
\label{sec:exp:metrics}

Fidelity $F$ is the Bell-pair fidelity after all purification, backbone, and correction operations, i.e.\ the $w$ component of the error vector at the schedule's root (Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}). Rate is $R=P_{\mathrm{success}}/T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$, where $P_{\mathrm{success}}$ is the probability that a complete schedule attempt succeeds and $T_{\mathrm{attempt}}$ is its deterministic wall-clock duration. Resource cost $C$ counts the number of \Gls{hrgs} copies (\texttt{Gen} leaves) consumed per trial; it is only the generation-cost component of the full resource budget $B=(n_{\mathrm{pur}},E_{\max},M_{\max})$ of Section~\ref{sec:model:budget}, not the whole tuple.

Two comparison modes recur throughout this chapter: \emph{matched-cost}, in which the search is constrained to the same generation cost as the paper schedule ($C=100$ at the reference configuration), and \emph{budget-relaxed}, in which it may return any schedule with $C\le E_{\max}$. Both modes share the same $E_{\max}$, $n_{\mathrm{pur}}$, and $M_{\max}$; only the returned schedule's own cost $C$ differs between them.

\section{Physics Validation}
\label{sec:exp:validation}

% Source: experiments/fig5_fidelity_vs_noise.py,
%          outputs/headline_experiment_n10/README.md

The evaluator described in Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator} reproduces the source paper's Fig.\,5~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} to four significant figures. At $e_d=0.01$, the three canonical schedules defined in Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce}, the raw chain, heralded end-node pumping, and optimistic end-node pumping, reach $F=0.82$, $0.92$, and $0.93$ respectively, matching the paper's own reported values of approximately $0.82$, $0.92$, and $0.93$. This agreement validates the physical model used by every search tier; it is not evidence that the search itself finds an optimal schedule.

Appendix~\ref{app:validation} gives the same comparison across the full swept range of $e_d$.

\section{Optimizer versus the Paper Schedule}
\label{sec:exp:comparison}

This section compares the paper schedule against schedules found by search: first at the source paper's own reference configuration $\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{ref}}$, then under network physics the paper does not model, and finally across a sweep of outer-photon noise.

\subsection{Headline Comparison}
\label{sec:exp:headline}

% Source: outputs/headline_experiment_n10/README.md,
%          outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

At $N=10$ and $e_d=0.01$, the paper schedule uses $C=100$ and achieves $F=0.93$ and $R=4055.92$. At matched cost, the optimizer returns $F=0.92$ and $R=4158.14$, a $2.5\%$ rate improvement obtained by accepting a lower but still feasible fidelity. Budget-relaxed, with same-span pumping enabled (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}), the optimizer returns $C=60$, $F=0.91$, and $R=6195.95$: it uses $60\%$ of the paper schedule's cost while achieving a $52.8\%$ higher rate. With pumping disabled, the search instead returns $C=50$, $F=0.90$, and an even higher rate, $R=6713.18$. This does not mean pumping should be disabled by default: pumping only ever adds candidates to the same beam-limited frontier (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}), so it can occasionally displace an unrelated non-pumped candidate that a narrower search would otherwise have kept, which is what happens here. Both settings are reported because both are used elsewhere in this thesis; Table~\ref{tab:headline} labels the four variants separately.

This matched-cost/budget-relaxed comparison is a first check, not the main claim of this chapter: the paper's own Fig.\,4 schedule is explicitly presented by its authors as one hand-picked example, so a systematic search improving on it is expected. What it establishes is that the search finds real, structurally valid, non-uniform schedules that behave as the physical model predicts, the basis for the stronger claims that follow.

\subsection{Network Generalization}
\label{sec:exp:netgen}

% Source: outputs/sweep_network_sensitivity/README.md

The headline comparison used the source paper's own idealized error model, in which inner-qubit measurements are noiseless ($p^X_{\mathrm{in}}=p^Z_{\mathrm{in}}=0$, Section~\ref{sec:model:network}). Introducing non-zero inner-qubit error, a form of noise the source paper does not model, changes which schedule actually works. At the same generation cost, the paper schedule can fall below the $F\ge0.9$ threshold entirely, while the search, re-run against the changed physics, finds a different, non-uniform schedule, one that spends its cost differently across hops rather than repeating one fixed circuit, that still clears it. This is the strongest result in this chapter: a case where the fixed hand-designed schedule stops working entirely and a schedule found by search does not.

Table~\ref{tab:netgen} and Figure~\ref{fig:netgen} compare the paper schedule with the optimizer under two non-ideal configurations at $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=100$, and $\gamma=0$: one with 18 \Gls{bsm} arms and one with 6, both with non-zero inner-qubit error rates rather than the source paper's own noiseless assumption. The reference (fully ideal) configuration was already given in Table~\ref{tab:headline}, where every schedule is feasible.

The main result here is a difference in feasibility, not a rate improvement: the paper schedule is infeasible in both non-ideal configurations, while the searched schedules remain above the fidelity threshold at the same cost. The infeasible paper schedule's raw rate is not a meaningful comparison point: a schedule that fails the fidelity floor is not an admissible answer to the optimization problem of Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives} regardless of its rate. The point is structural: a fixed, hand-designed schedule can silently stop working once the physical assumptions it was built for change, while a schedule constructed by search against the actual physics does not.

Branching factor is not used as a sensitivity axis in this chapter: within the model of Chapter~\ref{chap:model}, changing the tree branching factors does not affect $F$, $C$, or $R$. Hop length, unlike branching factor, is varied directly in Section~\ref{sec:exp:heterogeneous} below; uniformly rescaling every hop length by the same factor would multiply all schedule latencies by that factor and leave relative schedule comparisons unchanged, but the per-hop heterogeneous length tested there is a different, and more consequential, kind of variation.

\subsection{Per-Hop Heterogeneous Networks}
\label{sec:exp:heterogeneous}

% Source: outputs/random_network_adaptation/{30 seeds,100 seeds}/README.md,
%         src/hrgs_scheduler/models/random_network.py

Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen} changed the noise model but kept it uniform across hops. A real deployment instead has station spacing and hardware quality that vary hop to hop. This section tests that case directly using \texttt{random\_network\_config}, which draws each hop's length, inner-qubit error rates, and arm count independently, and asks whether a schedule found by search adapts to a network it was never specifically designed for. No adaptive or closed-loop mechanism is involved: the schedule is still fixed in advance for the whole configuration (Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}); only which fixed schedule the search selects depends on the given network.

A five-hop network with one hop set to a $12\times$ higher end-to-end inner-qubit error rate than its neighbours illustrates the effect concretely: at $E_{\max}=50$ and $F_{\min}=0.9$, no uniform link-level candidate clears the fidelity floor at any cost, while the schedule found by search clears it at a lower cost, by spending more generation nodes on the weak hop and fewer elsewhere (Table~\ref{tab:weaklink}). A uniform strategy is not merely suboptimal on this network: it is infeasible at every cost considered, while a schedule shaped differently per hop restores feasibility at lower cost.

    \small

This single designed case is confirmed on average over 100 independently random $N=6$ networks (\texttt{random\_network\_config}, seeds 0--99, $e_d=0.01$, $E_{\max}=60$, $F_{\min}=0.9$): among the networks with a feasible uniform link-level candidate, the optimizer's best feasible schedule improves rate by a mean of $20.37\%$ over that candidate, and never does worse. This is one of the two strongest results of this thesis, alongside the feasibility result of Section~\ref{sec:exp:netgen}: a schedule that adapts to per-hop network structure is, on average, a strictly better choice than a uniform one, without any closed-loop or adaptive mechanism, only a search run once against the specific network at hand.

\subsection{Noise Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:edsweep}

% Source: outputs/sweep_ed_n10/README.md

Sweeping $e_d$ from 0 to 0.01 at $N=10$ shows that the optimizer's rate advantage over the uniform link-level baseline grows with noise, from near zero at $e_d=0$ to $18.2\%$ at $e_d=0.01$. Figure~\ref{fig:edsweep} plots rate and fidelity against $e_d$ for the three variants of Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline}.

At low noise, the advantage is small because the fidelity floor is easy to satisfy and many schedules perform similarly (Section~\ref{sec:disc:nearoptimal}); the rate curves separate visibly only once $e_d$ exceeds about $0.004$. The fidelity panel shows the paper schedule's curve consistently above the matched-cost optimizer's: this is expected, not a discrepancy, since the matched-cost objective maximizes rate subject to $F\ge0.9$, not fidelity itself, so it spends the same cost on a schedule that clears the floor with less margin and a higher rate instead. The matched-cost fidelity curve is also not perfectly smooth: it jumps upward near $e_d=0.004$ because the best-ranked cost-$100$ schedule switches circuit family there (from a $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{YY}}$-only to a $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{XZ}}$-only sequence), and matched-cost always reports whichever cost-$100$ schedule currently ranks best, not a fixed schedule shape followed across the sweep. The budget-relaxed optimizer remains closest to the maximum rate observed at each noise level over most of the sweep, since it can spend less at low noise and reserve the freed cost for extra purification as noise increases.

\label{sec:exp:tradeoffs}

\subsection{Pareto Frontiers}
\label{sec:exp:pareto}

% Source: outputs/pareto_frontiers/README.md

At $N=10$ and $e_d=0.01$, with a permissive objective ($F_{\min}=0$, $E_{\max}=150$), the beam search evaluates 180 candidates. Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} plots two projections of the resulting frontier: fidelity against cost, and fidelity against rate. Each point is one evaluated schedule; grey points are Pareto-dominated (some other candidate is at least as good on both axes and strictly better on one), and the connected points form the non-dominated frontier the search actually has to trade against.

In the fidelity-cost projection, the paper schedule at $(C,F)=(100,0.93)$ lies strictly inside this frontier: a schedule at the same cost reaches $F\approx0.94$, a gain of about $0.006$ over the paper schedule's own fidelity at identical cost. This frontier is already close to flat by $C=60$: fidelity changes by less than $0.01$ all the way out to $C=140$, so within the searched schedules, spending well beyond the paper's own cost buys little further fidelity at this noise level. The paper schedule and the uniform link-level baseline are marked as individual points in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}.

\paragraph{Alternative objectives}
\label{sec:exp:objectives}
Changing only the optimization objective, maximizing fidelity subject to a rate floor, or minimizing cost subject to a fidelity floor, produces structurally different top-ranked schedules without changing the search algorithm itself. At $N=10$, $e_d=0.01$: maximizing fidelity under the paper's own rate floor reaches $F=0.94$ at $C=80$ (vs.\ $F=0.91$ at $C=60$ when maximizing rate); minimizing cost under only a fidelity floor returns $C=60$ but a rate as low as $R=1239.19$, since nothing in that objective prefers a higher-rate schedule at the same cost, unless a rate floor is also imposed, which restores $R=6195.95$ at the same $C=60$. Constraints must therefore reflect every performance requirement that matters to the intended use, not only the primary objective. Appendix~\ref{app:objectives} gives the full five-objective comparison.

\section{Scaling with Chain Length}
\label{sec:exp:scaling}

\subsection{Budget Scaling}
\label{sec:exp:budget}

% Source: outputs/sweep_min_budget_vs_n/README.md

For $N$ ranging from 10 to 28 (12 points tested, unevenly spaced to concentrate effort near the eventual crossover), the minimum $E_{\max}$ for which a schedule within the searched families reaches $F_{\min}=0.9$ is found by bisection: repeatedly halving the search interval on $E_{\max}$ and re-running the beam search at each trial value until the smallest feasible budget is pinned down. A descriptive power-law fit to the resulting points in log-log space gives
\[
    E_{\max}^{\min} \approx 1.369\,N^{1.620},
\]
with an exponent greater than one, indicating that the required budget grows faster over the measured range than the source paper's linear $10N$ budget formula. The paper's budget is first exceeded at $N=28$, where the minimum feasible budget is $E_{\max}=283$, just above $10\times28=280$; at $N=26$ the minimum feasible budget is $258$, just under $10\times26=260$. The crossover therefore lies between $N=26$ and $N=28$.

Three qualifications are important. First, the fitted power law is descriptive over the measured points and is not an asymptotic result. Second, the location of the crossover depends on the beam width and should therefore be regarded as provisional. Third, the excluded-construction result at $N=18$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp:excluded}) shows that a schedule outside the searched families can remain feasible at the paper's own budget. The fitted values therefore describe the minimum budget found within the searched schedule families, not a global lower bound over the full legal schedule space.

Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} collects supplementary sweeps over beam width, $e_d$, timing, and pumping that support this analysis without interrupting the main discussion. Among these, bisecting the minimum feasible budget over $e_d$ instead of $N$, at fixed $N=10$, shows that the paper's own fixed $E_{\max}=100$ overspends by $2.0$--$4.8\times$ across the tested noise range: the minimum budget needed to clear $F_{\min}=0.9$ ranges from $E_{\max}=21$ at low noise up to $E_{\max}=50$ at $e_d=0.01$. This is a stronger, self-contained claim than the $N$-scaling crossover above: it holds entirely within the paper's own tested configuration ($N=10$) and needs none of that crossover's beam-width caveats.

\subsection{Optimality Bound at $N=18$}
\label{sec:exp:excluded}

% Source: outputs/excluded_move_n14_n18/README.md,
%          docs/Optimality Scope.md

A hand-constructed schedule using the same-span construction excluded from the search (purifying two independently constructed $\mathrm{Span}(0,18)$ resources) achieves $F=0.93$ at $E_{\max}=180$, which is the paper's budget for $N=18$. The beam search cannot reach this schedule because that construction lies outside the schedule families considered by the search, even when same-span pumping is enabled (Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping}).

This is an optimality-scope result rather than a scalability result: it demonstrates that the searched families do not cover the full legal schedule space, consistent with the scope stated in Section~\ref{sec:model:scope}. It also provides a concrete example of why the bounded-search results must not be interpreted as proofs of global optimality.

\section{Timing Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp:timing}

% Source: outputs/sweep_gamma_and_tau_emit/README.md

Both axes studied here, memory dephasing and generation latency, affect a schedule according to its timing structure (where its \texttt{HeraldNode}s and \texttt{IdleNode}s sit, Section~\ref{sec:model:dag}), not according to its fidelity or resource cost.

\paragraph{Memory dephasing ($\gamma$)}

Sweeping $\gamma$ from 0 to $10^5$ at $N=10$ and $e_d=0.01$ leaves the raw chain and paper schedule's fidelity unchanged across the tested range: neither contains a resource that waits for another branch to become ready, so the simulated execution introduces no additional memory-decoherence interval for these schedules. By contrast, heralded end-node pumping falls from $F=0.92$ at $\gamma=0$ to $F=0.25$, the maximally mixed limit, at $\gamma=10^5$, because its sacrificial copies remain stored while each round-trip heralding delay is incurred.

The schedules selected by the optimizer remain flat across the tested values of $\gamma$. This does not mean that memory dephasing is generally harmless; rather, the search favours optimistic schedules in which the relevant intermediate heralding delays are deferred, avoiding the specific waiting pattern that causes the collapse of the heralded pumping schedule (Section~\ref{sec:disc:optimistic}).

\paragraph{Generation latency ($\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$)}

Sweeping $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}$ at $N=10$ and $\gamma=0$ reduces the rate monotonically for every schedule. The relative rate loss is largest for the optimistic pumping family, whose baseline latency is approximately $1\times L/c$, and smallest for heralded end-node pumping, whose baseline latency is approximately $9\times L/c$. A schedule that already has a larger fixed latency is proportionally less affected by the same additional per-photon generation delay. The two schedules cross near $\tau_{\mathrm{emit}}\approx0.01$; above this value, the larger fixed latency of heralded pumping makes it less sensitive, in relative terms, to further increases in generation latency.
