Three search tiers are implemented on top of one shared schedule representation and one shared evaluator. Whichever tier is under discussion, the schedule it returns is referred to informally throughout this thesis as \emph{the optimizer}; no single named software artefact carries that title, and Chapters~\ref{chap:results}--\ref{chap:discussion} state explicitly which tier produced a given result whenever it matters. Every returned candidate is a concrete schedule $\Sigma$: it passes the structural checks of Section~\ref{sec:model:legality} and is evaluated by the bottom-up pass from Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}. The tiers differ only in which legal \Glspl{dag} they consider. Search approximations can exclude a promising \Gls{dag} before final ranking, but they do not change how fidelity, success probability, latency, or cost is calculated for a \Gls{dag} that is returned.

The chapter moves from general search concepts to evaluation and then to increasingly broad but less exhaustive search. Section~\ref{sec:alg:prelims} introduces the three combinatorial-optimisation ideas used throughout the chapter. Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator} describes the shared evaluator. Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce} introduces the fixed baseline families, which are also retained as a safeguard against frontier pruning. Section~\ref{sec:alg:dp} presents the span-partition recurrence, Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping} introduces the additional same-span pumping operation and its search cap, and Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam} describes the beam-search heuristic used for the production-scale experiments in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\section{Preliminaries: Combinatorial Search Concepts}
\label{sec:alg:prelims}

The schedule space $\Sigma$ ranges over the legal \Glspl{dag} defined in Section~\ref{sec:model:dag} and is combinatorially large: even a modest chain admits combinatorially many legal schedules, far too many to enumerate one at a time. The three search tiers below borrow standard tools from combinatorial optimisation to make this space tractable. This section introduces each tool in general terms before it is applied to schedules specifically; a reader already familiar with Pareto optimality, dynamic programming, and beam search may skip ahead to Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator}.

\paragraph{Multi-objective optimisation and Pareto dominance} A search problem is \emph{multi-objective} when candidates are compared along several quantities at once rather than a single score, here cost $C$, fidelity $F$, and success probability $P_{\mathrm{success}}$. Candidate $A$ \emph{Pareto-dominates} candidate $B$ if $A$ is at least as good as $B$ in every quantity and strictly better in at least one; a candidate is \emph{Pareto-optimal} (non-dominated) if no other candidate dominates it, and the \emph{Pareto frontier} is the set of all non-dominated candidates~\cite{Pareto2014Manual,Deb2002Fast,Ehrgott2005Multicriteria}. Keeping the whole frontier, rather than a single best-scoring candidate, matters whenever a locally inferior choice can become the better choice after it is combined with another resource later, as occurs when sub-spans are swapped together.

\paragraph{Dynamic programming} \emph{Dynamic programming} solves a large problem by decomposing it into smaller and overlapping subproblems that can be solved once and reused wherever they recur (\emph{memoized})~\cite{Bellman1984Dynamic}. Section~\ref{sec:alg:dp} applies this idea to schedules: candidate sub-schedules for a short span are computed once and reused when constructing longer spans.

\paragraph{Heuristic and beam search} When the number of subproblems or candidates becomes too large for exhaustive enumeration, a \emph{heuristic search} trades completeness for tractability by discarding some candidates before they are fully explored. \emph{Beam search} is one such heuristic: at each stage it retains only a fixed number of candidates, the \emph{beam width}, according to a chosen ranking rule~\cite{Lowerre1976HARPY,Huang2018When,Meister2022BestFirst}. A narrower beam reduces computational cost but increases the chance of discarding a candidate that would have led to a better final schedule; increasing the beam width retains more of the search space at greater cost. Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam} applies this principle to the span-partition recursion of Section~\ref{sec:alg:dp}.

\section{The Schedule Evaluator}
\label{sec:alg:evaluator}

% Source: schedule/evaluator.py, experiments/fig5_fidelity_vs_noise.py

The evaluator makes one bottom-up pass over a schedule \Gls{dag}. At each node it applies the corresponding physical operation to the current Pauli-channel state, synchronizes branches that become ready at different times, applies the resulting memory decoherence, and propagates side effects and timing. At the root it returns fidelity $F$, success probability $P_{\mathrm{success}}$, resource cost $C$, and latency $L$. Rate is then $R=P_{\mathrm{success}}/L$ under the full-restart model of Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}.

Section~\ref{sec:exp:validation} shows that this pass reproduces the source paper's Fig.\,5 fidelity numbers to four significant figures. That agreement validates the physical model implemented by the evaluator; it does not establish that any search tier finds an optimal schedule, which is a separate question addressed by the remainder of this chapter and by Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\section{Fixed Schedule Families}
\label{sec:alg:bruteforce}

The first tier enumerates four fixed structural families: (1) a single raw end-to-end chain, with no purification; (2) end-node pumping, in which $n_{\mathrm{pur}}$ independent raw chains are purified sequentially at the end nodes with a round-trip herald between rounds (\emph{heralded} end-node pumping); (3) the optimistic counterpart, which defers classical heralding until the end (\emph{optimistic} end-node pumping); and (4) uniform link-level purification, applying the same copy count and circuit sequence at every hop. For even $N$ within budget, the Integrating paper's hand-chosen schedule~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} is included as a fifth fixed comparison point.

Three of these families, the raw chain, heralded end-node pumping, and optimistic end-node pumping, are referred to throughout this thesis as the \emph{canonical schedules}: they are the schedules whose fidelity is checked directly against the source paper's published Fig.\,5 benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:exp:validation}).

Within each family, purification sequences use the three two-copy circuits $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{YY}}$, $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{ZX}}$, and $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{XZ}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:bg:circuits}). All $3^r$ circuit sequences are enumerated up to a configured round cutoff $r_{\max}$; above that cutoff, the implementation uses a small curated set. Thus, ``brute force'' denotes exhaustive enumeration within these predefined structural families and circuit grid, not enumeration of every legal schedule \Gls{dag}.

These fixed families serve two purposes. First, they provide explicit baselines throughout Chapter~\ref{chap:results}: the raw chain and the source-paper schedule provide reference points, while uniform link-level purification represents a simple non-adaptive baseline that does not require any search over schedule structure. Second, their candidates are always added to the candidates produced by the two broader search tiers, so that these known schedule classes cannot be lost solely because of frontier pruning.

\section{Span Dynamic Programming}
\label{sec:alg:dp}

For every span $\mathrm{Span}(a,b)$, the second tier memoizes a Pareto frontier~\cite{Pareto2014Manual} of ways to create one resource over that span. A single-hop frontier ($b=a+1$) contains the raw link and link-level purification choices. For a wider span, the recurrence tries every split point $a<m<b$ and combines each retained candidate from $\mathrm{Span}(a,m)$ with each retained candidate from $\mathrm{Span}(m,b)$ using a Swap operation (Section~\ref{sec:bg:hrgs}). Each sub-span is computed once and reused wherever it appears in a wider construction. This reuse, rather than repeatedly constructing and evaluating complete end-to-end schedules, is the main computational advantage of the dynamic program.

The frontier retained at each span is multi-objective over $(C,F,P_{\mathrm{success}})$ (Pareto dominance, Section~\ref{sec:alg:prelims}). Only non-dominated candidates are retained, following the standard Pareto-pruning rule used in multi-criteria optimisation~\cite{Ehrgott2005Multicriteria}. This pruning is exact with respect to the three quantities tracked by the frontier, but it does not make the search itself exhaustive: the caps and heuristic retention rules introduced in the next two sections (same-span pumping, beam search) can still discard a non-dominated candidate before it reaches the end-to-end span.

\section{Same-Span Entanglement Pumping}
\label{sec:alg:pumping}

The span-partition recurrence alone constructs a resource by swapping together two different spans. It therefore does not represent repeated purification of a resource against a fresh resource at the same span. The second-tier extension adds this operation explicitly: given a candidate at span $\kappa$, it may consume that candidate together with an independently generated candidate at the same $\kappa$ and retain the purified output at $\kappa$. The operation is bounded by the configured copy-count and circuit limits rather than allowing an unrestricted number of pumping rounds.

An uncapped mode is available for targeted validation. It restores exhaustive pumping within the configured circuit and copy-count limits, but can already become impractical for small $N$ when the budget is large. It is therefore used as a diagnostic comparison rather than as the general production setting.

Pumping and ordinary Swap candidates compete for the same fixed-size frontier. Adding pumping can therefore remove a swap-only candidate from a smaller span before that candidate can contribute to an end-to-end frontier. An option to disable pumping entirely reproduces the earlier pumping-free search behaviour. Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline} reports both settings at the reference configuration because they can produce different top-ranked schedules.

\section{Beam Search}
\label{sec:alg:beam}

The third tier, beam search, reuses the same span recursion, node construction, and evaluator as Section~\ref{sec:alg:dp}. It caps every retained span frontier at a beam width $w_\text{beam}$, with $w_\text{beam}=25$ by default. This bounds runtime and memory at the network sizes studied in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} (up to $N=28$ hops), but a candidate that a wider beam would retain can be pruned before it reaches the end-to-end span. The cap is necessary in practice: an uncapped width-7 frontier already contains roughly $1{,}200$ candidates, making an uncapped search through the paper's $N=10$ chain impractical. The same $w_\text{beam}$ also caps the same-span pumping pool, so pumping and ordinary swap candidates share the available slots at each span.

At each span, the $w_\text{beam}$ retained slots are divided between two rankings. Roughly half retain the highest-fidelity candidates, preserving purified building blocks that may only become useful after later swaps. The remaining slots favour candidates that meet the fidelity hint $F_{\min}$, then rank them by higher success probability, higher fidelity, and lower cost. This split matters because a purely efficiency-based ranking would systematically reject link-level purification at short spans, where a raw link is locally cheaper and more likely to succeed. Accumulated noise across several unpurified raw links can nevertheless later make the end-to-end fidelity floor unattainable, so retaining some higher-fidelity candidates guards against that failure mode.

Throughout this thesis, a \emph{score} is the returned candidate's value of whichever quantity the active objective maximizes (Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}). Under the default rate-maximizing objective used for the beam search benchmarks of Table~\ref{tab:runtimes}, score is simply the candidate's rate $R(\Sigma)$.

Other heuristics could search the same space, but they would require a separate schedule encoding and proposal operators that preserve the \Gls{dag} legality conditions of Section~\ref{sec:model:legality}. The beam tier instead retains the span-partition representation and changes only how many candidates are kept at each frontier. This keeps all search tiers on the same physical evaluation path and makes repeated runs \emph{deterministic}. The resulting schedule is nevertheless only a valid candidate found by a bounded search, not a guarantee of global optimality.
