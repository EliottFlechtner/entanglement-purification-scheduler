Three search tiers are implemented on one shared schedule representation and evaluator. Whichever tier is under discussion, the schedule it returns is referred to informally throughout this thesis as \emph{the optimizer}; no single software artefact carries that name, and Chapters~\ref{chap:results}--\ref{chap:discussion} identify the tier used for a result whenever relevant. Every returned candidate is a concrete schedule $\Sigma$: it passes the structural checks of Section~\ref{sec:model:legality} and is evaluated by the bottom-up pass from Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}. The tiers differ only in which legal \Glspl{dag} they consider. Search approximations may exclude a promising \Gls{dag} before final ranking, but they do not change how fidelity, success probability, latency, or cost are calculated for a returned schedule.

The chapter progresses from general search concepts to evaluation and then to increasingly broad but less exhaustive search. Section~\ref{sec:alg:prelims} introduces the three combinatorial-optimisation ideas used throughout. Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator} describes the shared evaluator. Section~\ref{sec:alg:bruteforce} introduces the fixed baseline families, which are also retained as a safeguard against frontier pruning. Section~\ref{sec:alg:dp} presents the span-partition recurrence, Section~\ref{sec:alg:pumping} adds same-span pumping and its search cap, and Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam} describes the beam-search heuristic used for the production-scale experiments in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\section{Preliminaries: Combinatorial Search Concepts}
\label{sec:alg:prelims}

The schedule space $\Sigma$ ranges over the legal \Glspl{dag} defined in Section~\ref{sec:model:dag} and is combinatorially large: even modest chains admit too many legal schedules to enumerate individually. The three search tiers below use standard tools from combinatorial optimisation to make this space tractable. This section introduces them in general terms before applying them to schedules; readers familiar with Pareto optimality, dynamic programming, and beam search may skip to Section~\ref{sec:alg:evaluator}.

\paragraph{Multi-objective optimisation and Pareto dominance} A search problem is \emph{multi-objective} when candidates are compared using several quantities rather than a single score, here cost $C$, fidelity $F$, and success probability $P_{\mathrm{success}}$. Candidate $A$ \emph{Pareto-dominates} candidate $B$ if $A$ is at least as good in every quantity and strictly better in at least one. A candidate is \emph{Pareto-optimal} (non-dominated) if no other candidate dominates it, and the \emph{Pareto frontier} is the set of all non-dominated candidates~\cite{Pareto2014Manual,Deb2002Fast,Ehrgott2005Multicriteria}. Retaining the whole frontier rather than a single best candidate matters because a locally inferior choice can become preferable when combined with another resource later, as occurs when sub-spans are swapped together.

\paragraph{Dynamic programming} \emph{Dynamic programming} decomposes a large problem into smaller overlapping subproblems that are solved once and reused wherever they recur (\emph{memoized})~\cite{Bellman1984Dynamic}. Section~\ref{sec:alg:dp} applies this principle to schedules: candidate sub-schedules for a short span are computed once and reused when constructing longer spans.

\paragraph{Heuristic and beam search} When the number of subproblems or candidates becomes too large for exhaustive enumeration, a \emph{heuristic search} trades completeness for tractability by discarding candidates before they are fully explored. \emph{Beam search} is one such heuristic: at each stage it retains only a fixed number of candidates, the \emph{beam width}, according to a ranking rule~\cite{Lowerre1976HARPY,Huang2018When,Meister2022BestFirst}. A narrower beam reduces computational cost but increases the chance of discarding a candidate that would lead to a better final schedule; a wider beam retains more of the search space at greater cost. Section~\ref{sec:alg:beam} applies this principle to the span-partition recursion of Section~\ref{sec:alg:dp}.

\section{The Schedule Evaluator}
\label{sec:alg:evaluator}

% Source: schedule/evaluator.py, experiments/fig5_fidelity_vs_noise.py

The evaluator makes one bottom-up pass over a schedule \Gls{dag}. At each node it applies the corresponding physical operation to the current Pauli-channel state, synchronizes branches that become ready at different times, applies the resulting memory decoherence, and propagates side effects and timing. At the root it returns fidelity $F$, success probability $P_{\mathrm{success}}$, resource cost $C$, and latency $L$. Rate is then $R=P_{\mathrm{success}}/L$ under the full-restart model of Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}.

Section~\ref{sec:exp:validation} shows that this pass reproduces the source paper's Fig.\,5 fidelity numbers to four significant figures. This validates the physical model implemented by the evaluator, but does not establish that any search tier finds an optimal schedule, which is a separate question addressed by this chapter and Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\section{Fixed Schedule Families}
\label{sec:alg:bruteforce}

The first tier enumerates four fixed structural families:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a single raw end-to-end chain, with no purification;
    \item end-node pumping, in which $n_{\mathrm{pur}}$ independent raw chains are purified sequentially at the end nodes with a round-trip herald between rounds (\emph{heralded} end-node pumping);
    \item the optimistic counterpart, which defers classical heralding until the end (\emph{optimistic} end-node pumping); and
    \item uniform link-level purification, applying the same copy count and circuit sequence at every hop.
\end{itemize}
For even $N$ within budget, the Integrating paper's hand-chosen schedule~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} is included as a fifth fixed comparison point.

Three of these families, the raw chain, heralded end-node pumping, and optimistic end-node pumping, are referred to throughout this thesis as the \emph{canonical schedules}: they are the schedules whose fidelity is checked directly against the source paper's published Fig.\,5 benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:exp:validation}).

Within each family, purification sequences use the three two-copy circuits $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{YY}}$, $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{ZX}}$, and $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{XZ}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:bg:circuits}). All $3^r$ circuit sequences are enumerated up to a configured round cutoff $r_{\max}$; above that cutoff, the implementation uses a small curated set. Thus, ``brute force'' denotes exhaustive enumeration within these predefined structural families and circuit grid, not enumeration of every legal schedule \Gls{dag}.

These fixed families serve two purposes. First, they provide explicit baselines throughout Chapter~\ref{chap:results}: the raw chain and source-paper schedule provide reference points, while uniform link-level purification provides a simple non-adaptive baseline without structural search. Second, their candidates are always added to those produced by the two broader search tiers, preventing known schedule classes from being lost solely through frontier pruning.

\section{Span Dynamic Programming}
\label{sec:alg:dp}

For every span $\mathrm{Span}(a,b)$, the second tier memoizes a Pareto frontier~\cite{Pareto2014Manual} of ways to create one resource over that span. A single-hop frontier ($b=a+1$) contains the raw link and link-level purification choices. For a wider span, the recurrence tries every split point $a<m<b$ and combines each retained candidate from $\mathrm{Span}(a,m)$ with each from $\mathrm{Span}(m,b)$ using a Swap operation (Section~\ref{sec:bg:hrgs}). Each sub-span is computed once and reused in wider constructions. This reuse, rather than repeatedly constructing and evaluating complete end-to-end schedules, is the main computational advantage of the dynamic program.

The frontier retained at each span is multi-objective over $(C,F,P_{\mathrm{success}})$ (Pareto dominance, Section~\ref{sec:alg:prelims}). Only non-dominated candidates are retained, following the standard Pareto-pruning rule~\cite{Ehrgott2005Multicriteria}. This pruning is exact with respect to the three quantities tracked by the frontier, but does not make the search exhaustive: the caps and heuristic retention rules introduced in the next two sections can still discard a non-dominated candidate before it reaches the end-to-end span.

\section{Same-Span Entanglement Pumping}
\label{sec:alg:pumping}

The span-partition recurrence constructs a resource by swapping two different spans, and therefore does not represent repeated purification of a resource against a fresh resource at the same span. The second-tier extension adds this operation explicitly: given a candidate at span $\kappa$, it may consume that candidate together with an independently generated candidate at the same $\kappa$ and retain the purified output at $\kappa$. The operation is bounded by configured copy-count and circuit limits rather than allowing unrestricted pumping rounds.

An uncapped mode is available for targeted validation. It restores exhaustive pumping within the configured circuit and copy-count limits, but can become impractical for small $N$ when the budget is large. It is therefore used as a diagnostic comparison rather than the general production setting.

Pumping and ordinary Swap candidates compete for the same fixed-size frontier. Adding pumping can therefore remove a swap-only candidate from a smaller span before it contributes to an end-to-end frontier. An option to disable pumping entirely reproduces the earlier pumping-free search behaviour. Section~\ref{sec:exp:headline} reports both settings at the reference configuration because they can produce different top-ranked schedules.

\section{Beam Search}
\label{sec:alg:beam}

The third tier, beam search, reuses the same span recursion, node construction, and evaluator as Section~\ref{sec:alg:dp}. It caps every retained span frontier at a beam width $w_{\text{beam}}$, with $w_{\text{beam}}=25$ by default (Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps} justifies this choice). This bounds runtime and memory at the network sizes studied in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} (up to $N=28$ hops), but a candidate that a wider beam would retain can be pruned before reaching the end-to-end span. The cap is necessary in practice: an uncapped width-7 frontier already contains roughly $1{,}200$ candidates, making an uncapped search through the paper's $N=10$ chain impractical. The same $w_{\text{beam}}$ also caps the same-span pumping pool, so pumping and ordinary swap candidates share the available slots at each span.

At each span, the $w_{\text{beam}}$ retained slots are divided between two rankings. Roughly half retain the highest-fidelity candidates, preserving purified building blocks that may only become useful after later swaps. The remaining slots favour candidates that meet the fidelity hint $F_{\min}$, then rank them by higher success probability, higher fidelity, and lower cost. This split matters because a purely efficiency-based ranking would systematically reject link-level purification at short spans, where a raw link is locally cheaper and more likely to succeed. Accumulated noise across several unpurified raw links can nevertheless make the end-to-end fidelity floor unattainable, so retaining higher-fidelity candidates guards against this failure mode.

Throughout this thesis, a \emph{score} is the returned candidate's value of whichever quantity the active objective maximizes (Section~\ref{sec:model:objectives}). Under the default rate-maximizing objective used for the beam-search benchmarks of Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} in Appendix~\ref{app:sweeps}, score is simply the candidate's rate $R(\Sigma)$.
