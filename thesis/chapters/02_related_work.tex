\chapter{Related Work and State of the Art}
\label{chap:related}

This chapter reviews the three main areas of research on which this thesis builds: quantum repeater protocols, entanglement purification, and \Gls{rgs}-based architectures. It then places the scheduling gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:intro:gap} in the context of the existing state of the art.

\section{Quantum Repeater Protocols}
\label{sec:rw:repeaters}

Sending a photonic qubit directly through an optical fiber or free space is limited by the channel's transmissivity, i.e. the probability that a photon successfully reaches the receiver. For a channel of length $L$ and attenuation coefficient $\alpha$, this is given by $\eta=e^{-\alpha L}$. At the typical telecom C-band attenuation of $\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}\approx0.2~\mathrm{dB/km}$~\cite{ITUT2024Cable}, the transmissivity is already as low as $\eta\sim10^{-40}$ over 2000~km. Thus, even with perfect single-photon sources and detectors, direct transmission becomes impractical over long distances, with the achievable rate decreasing exponentially with distance~\cite{Sangouard2011Quantum}. More generally, the \emph{repeaterless secret-key capacity} of a lossy channel, bounded by the \Gls{plob}, exhibits the same exponential scaling in the high-loss regime~\cite{Pirandola2017Fundamental,Pant2017Ratedistance,Azuma2023Quantum}.

A quantum repeater~\cite{Briegel1998Quantum} divides a long channel into shorter elementary links, for which entanglement establishment is more feasible, then stitches the resulting short-range entanglement into a single end-to-end pair by entanglement swapping~\cite{Zukowski1993Eventreadydetectors,Grudka2008Entanglementswapping}. Repeater proposals are commonly grouped into three generations according to how they address photon loss and operational errors along the chain~\cite{Muralidharan2016Efficient,Azuma2023Quantum}. First-generation (1G) repeaters address both through \Gls{heg} and entanglement purification~\cite{Bennett1996Purification,Deutsch1996Quantum} (Section~\ref{sec:rw:purification}), storing successfully generated entangled pairs in matter-qubit memories while other links are established~\cite{Briegel1998Quantum,Duan2001Longdistance}. Second-generation (2G) repeaters replace entanglement purification with \Gls{qec} to protect against operational errors, while still relying on heralding to detect photon loss~\cite{Jiang2009Quantum,Wo2023Resourceefficient}. Third-generation (3G) repeaters use quantum error-correcting codes~\cite{Knill2000Theory,Gottesman1997Stabilizer} to protect against both loss and operational errors, avoiding the need to herald successful transmission across each elementary link~\cite{Fowler2010Surface,Azuma2015All}.

The all-photonic quantum repeaters~\cite{Azuma2015All} studied in this thesis belong to the third generation but use a different approach from most proposals in that category, which rely on matter-qubit error correction. Rather than protecting a stationary matter qubit, they encode the transmitted qubit itself in a multi-photon graph state~\cite{Briegel2001Persistent,Hein2006Entanglement,Hein2004Multiparty}: the \Gls{rgs}~\cite{Azuma2015All}. The tree encoding lets the protocol tolerate loss while operating on flying qubits, so no quantum memory is required at intermediate stations. Section~\ref{sec:rw:rgs} places this family among related architectures; Chapter~\ref{chap:background} gives the mechanics needed for this thesis.

A related but architecturally distinct proposal, the measurement-based quantum repeater~\cite{Zwerger2012Measurementbased}, keeps small pre-prepared entangled resource states~\cite{Raussendorf2002oneway,Raussendorf2002Computational,Raussendorf2003Measurementbased,Nielsen2006Clusterstate,Greenberger2009Compendium} at each station and couples them to incoming photons with simple \Glspl{bsm}, rather than building one graph state that spans the whole repeater chain~\cite{Browne2005Resourceefficient,Ewert20143,Zwerger2018Longrange}. This design's emphasis on minimising the qubit count held at each station is a useful point of comparison for the resource costs studied in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\section{Entanglement Purification Protocols}
\label{sec:rw:purification}

Chapter~\ref{chap:intro} already introduced the basic idea of trading copies for fidelity; this section covers the protocols that implement it. The two foundational two-qubit protocols, both using only \gls{locc}, are BBPSSW~\cite{Bennett1996Purification}, which operates on Werner states, and DEJMPS~\cite{Deutsch1996Quantum}, which extends the same approach to general Bell-diagonal states without first depolarizing them into Werner states through random local Pauli operations. Both protocols consume two copies and return one, succeeding only under the appropriate local measurement outcome condition: two units of resource cost are consumed to produce one output pair with increased fidelity.

Entanglement pumping describes a related idea: rather than purifying once and stopping~\cite{Briegel1998Quantum,Dur1999Quantum}, the same working pair is purified repeatedly, or \emph{pumped}, against a sequence of freshly generated pairs, increasing its fidelity further with each round~\cite{Dur1999Quantum,Deutsch1996Quantum,Bennett1996Purification}. Both ideas, the resource/fidelity trade-off and pumping, recur in the \Gls{rgs}-specific circuits on which this thesis' formal model is built (Section~\ref{sec:bg:circuits}).

Later work treats the purification circuit itself as an object to optimise rather than fixing it in advance. These approaches search directly over local Clifford operations to find shorter circuits with higher success probability and better output fidelity than the BBPSSW/DEJMPS family~\cite{Krastanov2019Optimized}, particularly when finite resources and imperfect local operations are taken into account. Purification has also been generalised from bipartite Bell pairs to multipartite graph states~\cite{Dur2003Multiparticle,Kruszynska2006Entanglement}. The \Gls{rgs}-specific two-to-one circuits used by the Integrating paper (Section~\ref{sec:bg:circuits}) adapt the same purification principles to the \Gls{rgs} architecture rather than directly implementing the original BBPSSW or DEJMPS protocols.

\paragraph{Photon loss tolerance} Purification addresses noise, not photon loss. Loss tolerance is nevertheless frequently discussed alongside purification in the photonic-repeater literature, so the distinction matters here. Tree encoding~\cite{Varnava2006Loss} provides a different form of redundancy: rather than consuming multiple copies of the same entangled pair to improve fidelity, it encodes one logical qubit in a tree-shaped graph state spanning multiple physical photons, whose branching factors set the achievable loss tolerance. The logical measurement outcome can then be reconstructed from the surviving branches. This approach was originally introduced for one-way measurement-based quantum computation~\cite{Raussendorf2003Measurementbased,Raussendorf2002oneway,Varnava2006Loss}. It was later specialized into a loss-tolerant quantum memory scheme~\cite{Varnava2007Loss}, along with an analysis of the resource-efficiency threshold at which such encoding becomes practical~\cite{Varnava2008How}. It is this tree encoding, rather than purification, that allows the inner qubits of the \Gls{rgs} to tolerate photon loss (Section~\ref{sec:bg:hrgs}).

\section{Repeater Graph State Architectures}
\label{sec:rw:rgs}

The \Gls{rgs} was proposed as the resource state for the first all-photonic quantum repeater: a graph state whose outer qubits provide the entangling links to neighbouring stations and whose inner qubits are tree-encoded~\cite{Varnava2006Loss}, allowing the logical connection to survive despite photon loss~\cite{Azuma2015All}. Later work has examined two recurring questions: how efficiently the \Gls{rgs} itself can be generated, and how much photon loss and operational error it can tolerate for a given resource budget. For generation, the original proposal constructs the graph state by fusing small entangled photon sets using linear optics~\cite{Azuma2015All,Pant2017Ratedistance}, while deterministic emitter-based schemes instead build the graph state directly from a small number of photon emitters which reduces the physical device count required per station~\cite{Borregaard2020OneWay,Li2022Photonic,Ghanbari2024Optimization}. These approaches have since been demonstrated experimentally at small scale~\cite{Thomas2024Fusion}. For loss and error tolerance, alternative graph topologies and adaptive measurement strategies can improve the entanglement rate for a fixed loss and error budget~\cite{Patil2024Improved,Li2025Generalized,Zhan2023Performance}; code-concatenated variants instead address loss and operational errors using two specialized codes rather than a single tree code~\cite{Wo2023Resourceefficient}.

The Bridging and Integrating papers, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro:gap} and formalised in Chapter~\ref{chap:background}, provide the specific starting point for this thesis: the former introduces the \Gls{hrgs} building block~\cite{Benchasattabuse2026Bridging,Benchasattabuse2026Resource,Benchasattabuse2024Architecture}, while the latter shows that purification can be incorporated into it using optimistic (blind) execution~\cite{Mobayenjarihani2023Optimistic,Benchasattabuse2025Integrating}. That feasibility result addresses whether purification can be integrated, but not how it should be scheduled; deciding where and how often to purify across a full chain is left open, which Section~\ref{sec:rw:positioning} returns to.

\section{Schedule Optimisation in Quantum Networks}
\label{sec:rw:scheduling}

Deciding when and where to apply entanglement purification is a separate problem, even once purification is available at every stage of a repeater chain. In adaptive schemes, a repeater node chooses its next operation based on fidelity or heralding outcomes observed so far, rather than following a fixed plan~\cite{Munro2015Quantum}. Reinforcement learning and other data-driven controllers have also been proposed for related quantum-network control problems. For example, one study reports substantial offline costs in richer settings, including state representations that grow exponentially, training times of up to $\sim 36$ hours per configuration, and hyperparameter searches required to outperform a heuristic baseline~\cite{Yau2026Reinforcement}. Optimistic purification has also been studied directly in generic quantum networks, independently of any \Gls{rgs}-specific construction~\cite{Mobayenjarihani2023Optimistic}, showing that deferring heralding can be useful beyond the architecture studied here. The choice of how to multiplex the qubits of a logical channel across serial or parallel physical links is another scheduling decision, with a measurable impact on end-to-end rate and fidelity~\cite{Meter2007Communication}.

\section{Positioning of This Work}
\label{sec:rw:positioning}

The work surveyed here either optimises a fixed component of a repeater design, such as a purification circuit or graph-state construction, or evaluates a specified scheduling policy. For the specific \Gls{hrgs} architecture studied in this thesis, no systematic search considers where to purify, how many copies to consume, which circuit to use, and in what order across a full chain. As Section~\ref{sec:intro:gap} establishes, this is the gap addressed by this thesis.
