\section{Quantum Repeater Protocols}
\label{sec:rw:repeaters}

Sending a photonic qubit directly through an optical fiber or free space is limited by the channel's transmissivity, i.e. the probability that a photon actually makes it through: $\eta = e^{-\alpha L}$, for a fiber of length $L$ and attenuation coefficient $\alpha$. At telecom C-band's typical $\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}\approx0.2~\mathrm{dB/km}$~\cite{ITUT2024Cable}, this already drops to $\eta\sim10^{-40}$ at 2000~km, so even with perfect single-photon sources and detectors, the odds of a photon surviving the trip become essentially zero, making direct transmission impractical over long distances. As a result, the achievable communication rate decreases exponentially with distance~\cite{Sangouard2011Quantum}. More generally, the \emph{repeaterless capacity} (or PLOB bound) of a lossy channel shows this same exponential decay in high-loss regimes~\cite{Pirandola2017Fundamental,Pant2017Ratedistance,Azuma2023Quantum}.

A quantum repeater~\cite{Briegel1998Quantum} divides a long channel into shorter elementary links, for which entanglement generation is more feasible, then stitches the resulting short-range entanglement into a single end-to-end pair by entanglement swapping~\cite{Zukowski1993Eventreadydetectors,Grudka2008Entanglementswapping}. Repeater proposals are commonly grouped into three generations according to how they address photon loss and operational errors along the chain~\cite{Muralidharan2016Efficient,Azuma2023Quantum}. First-generation (1G) repeaters address both through \gls{heg} and entanglement purification~\cite{Bennett1996Purification,Deutsch1996Quantum} (Section~\ref{sec:rw:purification}), storing successfully generated entangled pairs in matter-qubit memories while other links are established~\cite{Briegel1998Quantum,Duan2001Longdistance}. Second-generation (2G) repeaters replace entanglement purification with \gls{qec} to protect against operational errors, while still relying on heralding to detect photon loss~\cite{Jiang2009Quantum,Wo2023Resourceefficient}. Third-generation (3G) repeaters use quantum error-correcting codes~\cite{Knill2000Theory,Gottesman1997Stabilizer} to protect against both loss and operational errors, avoiding the need to herald successful transmission across each elementary link. This, in turn, eliminates the two-way communication latency associated with such heralding~\cite{Fowler2010Surface,Azuma2015All}. \TOFIX{review but probs leave out}{"distance-dependent" qualifying 2w latency}

The all-photonic quantum repeaters~\cite{Azuma2015All} studied in this thesis belong to the third generation but use a different approach from most matter-qubit code-based proposals: rather than protecting a stationary matter qubit, they encode the transmitted qubit itself in a multi-photon graph state~\cite{Briegel2001Persistent,Hein2006Entanglement,Hein2004Multiparty}: the \gls{rgs}~\cite{Azuma2015All}. This allows loss and error correction to directly happen on flying qubits and requires no quantum memory at any intermediate station. Section~\ref{sec:rw:rgs} reviews the different ways this family of proposals has actually been built; its exact mechanics are only formalised in Chapter~\ref{chap:background}.

A related but architecturally distinct proposal, the measurement-based quantum repeater~\cite{Zwerger2012Measurementbased}. Rather than building one graph state that spans the whole repeater chain~\cite{Zwerger2018Longrange}, it keeps small pre-prepared entangled resource states~\cite{Raussendorf2002oneway,Raussendorf2002Computational,Raussendorf2003Measurementbased,Nielsen2006Clusterstate,Greenberger2009Compendium} at each station and couples them to incoming photons with simple \glspl{bsm}~\cite{Browne2005Resourceefficient,Ewert20143}. This design's emphasis on minimising the qubit count held at each station is a useful point of comparison for the resource costs studied in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\section{Entanglement Purification Protocols}
\label{sec:rw:purification}

Entanglement purification takes several noisy copies of an entangled pair and by using only \gls{locc} returns fewer copies of higher fidelity. The two founding two-qubit protocols are BBPSSW~\cite{Bennett1996Purification}, which purifies against a fixed depolarised noise model, and DEJMPS~\cite{Deutsch1996Quantum}, which extends the same idea to general Bell-diagonal states without first forcing the state into that depolarised form by applying random local Pauli operations. Both protocols consume two copies and return one, succeeding only when the two parties' local measurement outcomes agree: two units of resource cost \TOFIX{choose}{exchanged/bought/traded} for one unit of fidelity gain.

Entanglement pumping describes a related idea: rather than purifying once and stopping~\cite{Briegel1998Quantum,Dur1999Quantum}, the same working pair is repeatedly purified (\emph{pumped}) in a nested sequence against freshly generated pairs, restoring its fidelity a little further each round~\cite{Dur1999Quantum,Deutsch1996Quantum,Bennett1996Purification}. Both ideas, the resource/fidelity trade-off and pumping, recur in the \gls{rgs}-specific circuits this thesis' formal model is built on (Section~\ref{sec:bg:circuits}).

Later work treats the purification circuit \emph{itself} as an object to optimise rather than to fix in advance. These approaches search directly over local Clifford operations to find shorter circuits with higher success probability and better output fidelity than the BBPSSW/DEJMPS family~\cite{Krastanov2019Optimized} -- particularly once finite resources and imperfect local operations are taken into account. Purification has also been generalised from bipartite Bell pairs to general multipartite graph states~\cite{Dur2003Multiparticle,Kruszynska2006Entanglement}; the RGS-specific two-to-one circuits used by the Integrating paper (Section~\ref{sec:bg:circuits}) come from this graph-state generalisation, rather than from the original two-qubit BBPSSW/DEJMPS protocols directly.

\section{Repeater-Graph-State Architectures}
\label{sec:rw:rgs}

The \gls{rgs} was proposed as the resource state for the first all-photonic quantum repeater: a graph state whose outer qubits carry the entangling links to neighbouring stations and whose inner qubits are tree-encoded (Section~\ref{sec:rw:purification}), ensuring the logical connection survives even when most physical photons are lost~\cite{Azuma2015All}. Follow-up work has been mainly about optimizing two aspects of this proposal: how cheaply the \gls{rgs} itself can be generated, and how much loss and operational error it can tolerate for a given resource budget. On generation, the original proposal fuses small entangled photon sets with linear optics~\cite{Azuma2015All,Pant2017Ratedistance}, while deterministic emitter-based schemes instead build the graph state directly from a small number of photon emitters, reducing the physical device count needed per station~\cite{Borregaard2020OneWay,Li2022Photonic,Ghanbari2024Optimization}. These have since been demonstrated experimentally at small scale~\cite{Thomas2024Fusion}. On tolerance, alternative graph topologies and adaptive measurement strategies improve the entanglement rate for a fixed loss and error budget~\cite{Patil2024Improved,Li2025Generalized,Zhan2023Performance}; code-concatenated variants target loss and operational error with two separate, specialised codes rather than a single tree code~\cite{Wo2023Resourceefficient}.

The Bridging paper introduces the \gls{hrgs} building block used throughout this thesis, which splits the \gls{rgs} construction across two stations instead of requiring the whole $2m$-qubit state to exist in one place~\cite{Benchasattabuse2026Bridging,Benchasattabuse2026Resource,Benchasattabuse2024Architecture}. Its main consequence is architectural rather than purely photonic: because a \gls{hrgs} only needs to be generated and briefly held, an end node equipped with a single quantum memory can act as its \emph{own source}. This lets the all-photonic \gls{rgs} scheme interoperate with conventional memory-equipped repeater nodes instead of requiring every intermediate station to be purely photonic~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating,Benchasattabuse2026Bridging}. Chapter~\ref{chap:background} formally describes this architecture and its resulting station roles.

\TOFIX{rephrase}{The Integrating paper then asks a question the \gls{rgs} literature has so far left open: whether purification can be inserted into this scheme at all, given that an \gls{rgs} is normally generated and consumed within a single fast round-trip that a classical purification herald would otherwise have to interrupt~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating}. Its answer, optimistic (blind) purification~\cite{Mobayenjarihani2023Optimistic,Benchasattabuse2025Integrating} performed directly on \gls{hrgs} primitives without waiting for heralding outcomes, is what Section~\ref{sec:bg:optimistic} formalises, and is also what makes a purification schedule for this architecture a genuinely open combinatorial question rather than a fixed formula, which Section~\ref{sec:rw:positioning} returns to.}

\section{Schedule Optimisation in Quantum Networks}
\label{sec:rw:scheduling}

Deciding when and where to apply entanglement purification is a separate problem, even once it is available at every stage of a repeater chain. In adaptive schemes, a repeater node chooses its next operation from the fidelity or heralding outcomes observed so far, rather than following a fixed plan~\cite{Munro2015Quantum}. Reinforcement learning and other data-driven controllers have also been proposed for related quantum-network control problems, but come with significant offline cost: state representations that grow exponentially in richer settings, training times of up to $\sim 36$ hours per configuration, and an \TOFIX{recheck from abstract of the CHINESE paper}{ad-hoc hyperparameter search often needed per distance/configuration} to guarantee the policy beats the heuristic baseline~\cite{Yau2026Reinforcement}. Closer to the non-adaptive setting this thesis studies, optimistic entanglement purification has also been studied directly in generic quantum networks, independently of any \gls{rgs}-specific construction~\cite{Mobayenjarihani2023Optimistic}, showing that deferring heralding is useful well beyond the \gls{rgs}-based architecture studied here. The choice of how to multiplex the qubits of a logical channel across serial or parallel physical links is itself also a scheduling decision, with a measurable impact on end-to-end rate and fidelity~\cite{Meter2007Communication}.

\TOFIX{maybe move in next section 'positioning'?}{None of this prior work, however, performs a systematic search over the schedule space of an \gls{hrgs}-based repeater. The closest starting point, the Integrating paper, demonstrates that purification can be placed at the generation, link, or end-node stage of an \gls{hrgs} chain, and mixed freely between them. The paper demonstrates this only through a single hand-picked example schedule; it explicitly leaves the search for a better schedule, given a fixed physical network and resource budget, as an open problem~\cite{Benchasattabuse2025Integrating}. That is the gap this thesis addresses.}
